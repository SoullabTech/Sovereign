# The Game of Liberation — *sa gnon rnam bzhags*

Schlieter (2012) studies the Tibetan Buddhist board game **"ascending the
[spiritual] levels"**, ascribed to Sa skya paṇḍita Kun dga' rgyal mtshan
(1182–1251), as a **soteriological simulation** rather than a divinatory
device. The game belongs to the *Chutes and Ladders* family that originated
in medieval India (Jain *Gyan Chaupar*, Hindu *mokṣa-paṭa*).

**Classification: secondary academic analysis.** Schlieter is an outside
scholar; the game itself is the artifact of the tradition.

## What the game does

- Simulates the spiritual paths of the three major Buddhist vehicles
  (hearers, bodhisattvas, tantric adepts), and the cosmologies of Hindu, Bon,
  and Muslim traditions
- Depending on the throw of a die, the player moves through realms (hells,
  animal, asura, human, deva) and along paths (Sūtra, Tantra) toward the
  *Dharmakāya* and *nirvāṇa*
- Encodes a specific Mahāyāna view: only the two Mahāyāna paths
  (bodhisattva, vajra) reach final liberation; other paths are blind alleys

## Key structural features

- **The Vajra Hell (square 1)** — entered from square 33 (Tantric Path of
  Accumulation) when one's die rolls a 6, modeling the consequences of
  broken samaya
- **The point of no return (square 66)** — entry to the first Tantra level;
  past this, the player cannot leave the Tantric path
- **Row 13** — the hagiographic life of Buddha Śākyamuni, traversed
  one-square-at-a-time to enter *nirvāṇa*

## What the game implies about karma

Schlieter argues that the game does **not** straightforwardly endorse a
mechanical view of karma. Because the die is pure chance and the same player
can play repeatedly with different outcomes, the game subtly **undermines**
the "causal" reading of karma as immediate, transparent retribution. This
matches Sa skya paṇḍita's own critique (verses 156–157):

> "That teaching called 'the inevitable effectiveness of light and dark
> deeds' is widely hailed as a great wonder. / Yet it simply mistakes an
> interpretable sense for one that is definite."

## Why this is in the folder

- Useful as a structural map of how the tradition itself articulates
  hierarchies of paths, dangers of vows, and the relation of chance to karma
- Offers a frame for talking about **paths** without imposing one
- Provides academic vocabulary that MAIA may use when discussing the
  Vajrayāna's internal logic with a member

## What MAIA may do

- Reference the game by name when a member is asking about how Tibetan
  Buddhism understands paths and karma.
- Cite Schlieter as the academic source.
- Use the *airplane vs. horse* image (Thondup) and the Vajra-hell rule to
  illustrate why tantric vows are held with such gravity.

## What MAIA must not do

- Use the game as a divinatory device for the member.
- Use it to suggest that a member is "on" any particular path.
- Flatten the three vehicles into "stages" of one path.
