# Saint John of the Cross — Paschasius (1919)

A 1919 Discalced Carmelite biography of John of the Cross by Fr. Paschasius Heriz, O.C.D. Provides the **life** behind the texts: his upbringing in poverty, his collaboration with Teresa of Ávila in the Carmelite reform, his imprisonment in Toledo (where much of the *Spiritual Canticle* was composed in his head), his role as spiritual director, and his death.

> Note: This is a devotional / hagiographic biography from within the Carmelite tradition, not a critical modern biography. It is included for the **lived shape** of John's life and direction-work, not as a neutral historical source.

## Structural shape

A continuous biographical narrative organised around John's early life, his entry into Carmel, the reform movement with Teresa, the conflict with the Calced Carmelites, his imprisonment, his subsequent foundations, and his final illness and death. Interwoven with reflections on his spiritual teaching.

## Representative passages

### On suffering and conformity to the will of God

> John spoke to them of the hidden graces of poverty, of the merit of suffering and conformity to the will of God, with so much unction that the fathers left the refectory with their hearts on fire.

### On his bearing under violence

> The assassin fell upon him and with a stick gave him such terrible blows that St. John fell on the ground badly wounded, but very happy to suffer something for the love [of God].

## How MAIA may use this

- For **biographical grounding** when retrieving from John's primary texts — situating the writing in a life of imprisonment, reform, marginalisation, and direction.
- As a reference when members ask *who* John of the Cross was, not only what he wrote.
- To distinguish his **lived practice** (small communities, manual work, slow spiritual direction) from later popularised versions of "the dark night."

## Use boundary

The hagiographic register of this text — its glorification of suffering, its readiness to read every difficulty as grace — is a **source artifact, not MAIA's stance**. MAIA must never echo the suggestion that a member's suffering is itself good or willed by God. That reading belongs only to the person living it, if they choose it.
