## §1 Why this document exists

Any institutional review of a practitioner field — a positive-psychology faculty evaluating the Studio for newly certified coaches, a certifying body, a serious practitioner doing due diligence — will ask two questions, roughly in this order:

1. **"What happens when a client is in trouble?"**
2. **"Where does this system's scope of practice end?"**

These are good questions and they deserve a prepared, honest answer — not improvisation in the room, and not a policy page written after being asked. This document is that answer, held to the claim discipline (`docs/canon/MARKETING_CLAIM_DISCIPLINE.md`): every statement below is stamped **Live**, **Designed**, or **Vision**, and the Failure Test is applied to the whole.

The companion document for the second predictable question (outcomes) is `docs/pitch/OUTCOMES_QUESTION_VISION_ENTRY_2026-07-10.md`.

## §2 Scope of practice — what MAIA is and is not, structurally

**Live.** The scope boundary is not a policy statement; it is enforced structure:

- **Non-clinical by architecture.** MAIA does not diagnose, does not model the person, does not claim certainty about interior states. These are refusals in the Refusal Registry (`docs/architecture/REFUSAL_REGISTRY.md`), not guidelines — the system is built to be unable to do these things well, and to decline rather than approximate them.
- **The constitutional floor sits above everything a practitioner authors.** Direction of Authority (Invariant 16, `docs/canon/MAIA_SOVEREIGNTY_INVARIANTS.md`): a practitioner field *narrows* what MAIA may do beneath the floor; it can never widen it. Enforced in `lib/practiceField/fieldGuidance.ts`. A practitioner cannot — even accidentally, even with the best framework — author MAIA into clinical territory.
- **Companion, never the practitioner.** The field speaks the practitioner's articulation between sessions; at its edges it defers *to the practitioner*, by design. The system's own boundary language routes the person back to their human.
- **Rooms end.** Off-ramps are live: the room has a closing gesture and hands the person back to their life. There is no design pressure toward open-ended engagement — the Sovereignty Invariants prohibit attachment capture outright.
- **Sanctuary Mode.** A member can speak without memory formation, unconditionally. Vulnerability is never converted into data against the member's wishes.

**The honest sentence for the review room:** *MAIA's scope of practice is enforced by what the system structurally cannot do, not by what a terms-of-service page says it won't.*

## §3 Crisis — the honest current answer

This is where honesty matters most, because the tempting answer is the dangerous one.

**What we do NOT claim (and why the non-claim is load-bearing):**

- **No crisis-detection capability is claimed.** MAIA has no clinical triage system, no risk-scoring of members, no automated escalation pipeline. We refuse to claim detection we do not have, because a *claimed* safety net that doesn't reliably exist is worse than a clearly named boundary — it transfers vigilance away from the humans who actually hold it. (This is the platform's own "dangerous failure feels like success" discipline applied to safety: a quiet false-negative behind a confident claim is the worst outcome available.)
- **Person-modeling is refused, and crisis-detection *is* person-modeling.** A system that scores members for risk is surveilling their interiors — the exact thing the Monitor quadrant fence (spec §3.4: *"the field's health, never the clients' interiors"*) and the privacy model exist to prevent. The refusal is principled, not a gap we haven't gotten to.

**What IS true today (Live):**

- MAIA's register under distress is governed by the constitutional floor: no diagnosis, no interpretation of what pain "means," no simulated therapeutic authority, deferral to the human practitioner at the edges of the field.
- The structural posture — reflection, member's own language, agency-preserving questions — is the posture least likely to *deepen* harm in a vulnerable moment: MAIA does not tell a person in trouble what their trouble means.
- The practitioner relationship is the named container. The client of a practitioner field arrived through a human professional who holds duty of care; MAIA's deferral is *to that specific human*, not to a generic disclaimer.

**What is Designed (specified or partially built, not yet claimable as live):**

- Field-authored referral language: the practitioner, in the charter session, articulates in their own voice where their practice's edge is and where they send people (their supervision arrangements, their referral network, crisis lines for their jurisdiction). This composes into the field like everything else the practitioner authors — the crisis answer inherits the same provenance discipline as the rest of the corpus. **Folded in (Kelly directive, 2026-07-10):** this is now Question V of the Field Charter (`docs/fields/FIELD_CHARTER_TEMPLATE.md`) — every charter session produces the practitioner's crisis answer as a side effect. Remains Designed (not Live) until a charter authored under the five-question template composes into a production room.

**What is Vision (named, not designed):**

- Any structured hand-off surface (e.g., a member-visible "reach my practitioner" gesture inside a field room) — plausible, consonant with the architecture, **not designed, not authorized here**.

**The honest sentence for the review room:** *MAIA does not claim to detect crisis — we believe claimed detection without clinical reliability is itself a harm. What it does instead is structural: it cannot diagnose, cannot interpret, defers to the human practitioner at its edges, ends its rooms, and carries the practitioner's own referral language as part of the authored field. The safety model is the practitioner's duty of care, extended — never replaced.*

## §4 The three predictable follow-ups, answered

**"But what if a client discloses suicidal ideation to MAIA at 2am?"**
Honest answer: MAIA's floor prevents the harmful responses (interpretation, false reassurance, simulated therapeutic authority) and its authored field carries the practitioner's own referral and crisis-line language (once §3-Designed lands; today, the floor's deferral posture is the live behavior). What MAIA does not do is silently score the disclosure or alert anyone — because the member was never told they were being monitored, and stealth monitoring is a consent violation this platform is constitutionally unable to commit. The practitioner sets expectations with their client about what MAIA is, using `docs/practitioner/CLIENT_EXPLANATION.md`. If a reviewer believes disclosure-triggered escalation is mandatory for their context, that is a real conversation about *their* program's requirements — and it may mean their context needs features this platform will not build, which is a legitimate outcome. We would rather lose the deployment than claim safety machinery we don't have.

**"Who is liable?"**
The practitioner holds their existing professional duty of care; the platform holds its constitutional guarantees (non-diagnosis, consent, provenance, deferral). The line between those is exactly the line the architecture enforces. Jurisdiction-specific liability language is Kelly's + counsel's to author — flagged here as **open, owned, and not improvised**.

**"Has this been reviewed by a clinician?"**
Honest answer today: no. The constitutional design is strong on non-harm-by-structure; it has not had formal clinical review. If institutional deployment is pursued, inviting that review *early* is the move consistent with how this platform treats verification — the review is evidence to earn, not a checkbox to claim.

## §5 Scope discernment as progressive development

The reviewer's unstated premise deserves a direct answer: the risk profile of a *newly certified coach* is the inverse of the therapist-turned-coach's. A therapist brings clinical discernment and needs a platform; a certificate-holding coach brings a framework, and the discernment — recognizing when the thing in front of him isn't a flourishing problem, feeling where his depth ends *before* he's past it — is exactly what certification does not confer. It develops the way it developed in every clinician: supervised contact with boundary cases. Most coaches never get that. This platform's honest account is that it is built for that gap — in three layers, each stamped.

**The floor is the client-safety guarantee during the practitioner's own maturation (Live).** MAIA's constitutional floor — no diagnosis, no person-modeling, no simulated therapeutic authority, rooms that end — does not depend on the practitioner's maturity, because the field composes *beneath* the floor (Inv 16, enforced in `lib/practiceField/fieldGuidance.ts`). A novice coach's field cannot make the room do the harmful things, however green his judgment. The platform is safe for his clients on day one of his *practice*, not day one of his *wisdom*. This is load the floor was always carrying; the novice-coach profile just makes it visible.

**The rehearsal library as scope-discernment curriculum (Vision — named, not designed; builds with the Studio's rehearsal stage, `docs/specs/PRACTITIONER_FIELD_ADMIN_SPEC_2026-07-10.md` §3.2).** When the Studio's sandbox rehearsal exists, its scenario library should deliberately include the **out-of-scope cases** — the client whose stuckness is grief, the disclosure that isn't a values problem, the session where flourishing language is papering over something clinical. The practitioner rehearses against those without live stakes, reads his own room's transcripts, and develops the recognition — *this is the shape of a thing that isn't mine* — before any real client brings it. That is how clinical discernment forms in supervision: exposure to boundary cases inside a reflective frame. No coach training program offers it at this fidelity; this platform can, and the design intent is recorded here so the scenario library is built as a curriculum, not a demo reel.

**Question V as a versioned, revisited artifact (Designed — versioning substrate built, PR #586 pending merge).** The practitioner's first answer to *"where does your practice end, and where do you send people?"* will be immature — a certificate-holder's answer, too confident or too vague. That is acceptable **if it is version 1**. Charter answers compose into the practice field, and every field save now writes an append-only revision (`practice_field_revisions`, migration `20260710000002`): revisited after months of transcripts and rehearsals, version 3 of his scope answer is a different document — and the diff *is* his professional maturation on the dimension that matters most, readable to himself. The versioning spine was framed as protecting the steward baseline; its deeper purpose is this: it makes the growth of the practitioner's judgment inspectable, to the practitioner.

**The line the platform must not cross, restated for this exact temptation:** the system cannot *detect* the out-of-scope client for him. That is person-modeling, refused in §3 on principle — and it would also arrest the very development named here, because a coach whose system flags the clinical cases never develops the discernment himself. The platform structures his *practice of recognizing*; it never substitutes for the recognition. This is the Mirror Invariant applied to the practitioner's own becoming: the system holds the rehearsal room; he does the seeing.

**The honest sentence for the review room:** *The platform does not certify a coach's judgment or compensate for its absence — it makes clients structurally safe while that judgment matures, gives the practitioner a supervised-practice analogue for developing it, and keeps a version history in which its growth is visible.*

## §6 The practitioner spectrum — what varies, what's invariant

**Status of this whole section: Vision — a named direction, held (Cat 1). Nothing here is designed or authorized. It exists so the panel-facing document already shows the platform knows the difference between a coach and a clinician.**

The practitioner range this platform can eventually hold runs from the newly certified coach (§5's profile — the reference implementation) to the licensed clinician. The design question is not whether to serve that spectrum but **what varies across it and what does not** — because getting the split wrong in either direction breaks the platform's integrity.

**What stays invariant end to end: the floor — and the scope of the work itself.** A licensed clinician's field does *not* get a MAIA that diagnoses. The floor governs what MAIA may do, and the Mirror Invariant does not relax for credentials. But the invariant runs deeper than MAIA's behavior (**Kelly ruling, 2026-07-10**): *even when the practitioner is a psychologist, diagnosis and therapy are not in the scope of this work.* The work practiced on this platform — the field, the room, the between-sessions companionship — is developmental, for every practitioner on the spectrum. A license does not convert a practice field into a clinical instrument; it changes what the practitioner may lawfully do *in her own clinical practice, outside this container*, and therefore what she can receive into when the work reaches its edge. If the spectrum were implemented as "clinicians unlock a less-restricted MAIA" — or a less-restricted *field* — the platform would have rebuilt the person-modeling machine with a credential as the key, and every constitutional argument in §3 collapses. The named failure mode this ruling forecloses is **capability inheritance through authorship**: a field becoming clinically capable because a clinician wrote it, her license flowing into the room through the corpus — the same channel every practitioner's teleology flows through — so the room performs an authority its author holds but the container doesn't, with the harm arriving disguised as credentials rather than confabulation. The floor and the work's scope are the spectrum's fixed points; everything that varies, varies above them.

**The scope-invariance also removes a gatekeeping function the platform should never hold.** If licenses modulated the work, the platform would have to verify credentials, tier them, and police the boundary — the attestation posture (point 3 below) metastasized into adjudication. With the work invariant, credentials matter only to the hand-off destination the practitioner names in Question V: *their* authored answer, their liability frame, their container. The platform holds one kind of work for everyone and lets the edges be authored. This is jurisdiction-not-verdict applied to the professional spectrum entire.

**What genuinely varies — four things:**

1. **Question V inverts.** For a coach, "where does your practice end?" points *outward* — the clinical need he refers away. For a clinician it points inward and outward differently: crisis is inside her *professional* competence (though never inside the platform work's scope — see the invariant above), and her practice still has edges (psychiatry, specializations, jurisdiction). The five-question charter holds for both; only V's answer-shape differs. The charter template can carry exemplar answers per practitioner class rather than different questions.

2. **Crisis posture differentiates by destination, not architecture.** §3's "MAIA claims no detection" holds universally. What the off-ramp *reaches* differs: a coach's room hands off to his referral network; a clinician's room hands off to *the clinician herself* — but note what that hand-off is: a crossing out of the platform's developmental scope into her clinical practice, a container change even though the human is the same. She receives it under her license, in her clinical setting, not inside the field. Same deferral structure, different destination — which Question V already captures as authored field content.

3. **Attestation, held to the claim discipline.** The platform can record **attested** credentials with provenance ("license number as stated by the practitioner, on date, unverified" — versus verified-by-registry if that is ever built) — but it must never *display* a verification it did not perform. A "Licensed Clinician" badge the platform cannot back is the invented-claim failure at professional scale. Attested-not-verified is the honest v1 tier, labeled as such.

4. **Support depth scales inversely with license.** The coach end needs more platform scaffolding — the rehearsal library, the scope-discernment scenarios (§5), the formation the certificate did not confer. The clinician end needs less formation and more instrumentation — documentation trails, supervision artifacts, and cleaner boundaries around what the platform is (a between-sessions companion adjunct to treatment, never treatment). The Studio's quadrants serve both; the emphasis inverts.

**The spectrum is an ecology, not a tier list.** Coaches refer up; clinicians receive. If both ends live on the platform, a coach's Question V answer can eventually *name a clinician who is also here* — the referral graph becomes real infrastructure, and the platform holds the helping-professions range as a relational medium rather than a market segmentation. This is the Developmental Ecology layer appearing on the practitioner side.

**One strategic dividend, connecting to §4's honest "no":** the first licensed clinician onboarded *is* the clinical reviewer this document says has not yet been invited. Recruiting the clinician end of the spectrum is not market expansion first — it is the platform's clinical review, arriving as a practitioner.

**Sequencing, per the house's own rule:** the coach end is being walked now (Larry as reference implementation). The clinician tier gets designed from *a clinician's* actual contact — not extrapolated from a coach's. Nothing in this section authorizes a build.

**The honest sentence for the review room:** *The platform does not verify licenses — it is built so that verifying them is not load-bearing: the work is developmental for every practitioner who holds it, credentials matter only at the exit door the practitioner authors, and no credential converts the instrument into a clinical one.*
