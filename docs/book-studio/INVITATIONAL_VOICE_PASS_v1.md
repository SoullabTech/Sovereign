## Diagnosis

**Where the book is already invitational** (most of Part One):
- The "we" voice in the Preface, Ch3, much of Ch2 reads as collective.
- Personal anecdote (campfire, son, dream of the elements) is shared
  experience, not instruction.

**Where it tilts toward instruction** — five recurring shapes:

1. **Visualization prompts in command voice.** *"Imagine X. Now picture Y. Visualize Z."* The image is offered, but framed as the reader's homework rather than something Kelly is sitting with too.

2. **"You will / You'll experience" futures.** Promises transformation, which implies the reader hasn't already.

3. **"If you want to X, you must Y" constructions.** Conditional commands.

4. **"By doing X, you will Y" cause-and-effect.** Assumes the reader hasn't been doing X.

5. **Section closers that direct how to read.** *"Think of these pages as..."* — telling the reader how to receive what's on the page.

## Stiffness-reduction patterns *(working reference for ongoing prose)*

> Kelly's request: "make it feel less stiff and more inviting, open
> and immersive." These are recurring micro-moves the prose can
> use whenever the voice tightens. Not rules — patterns that the
> sentences themselves seem to ask for, once you start listening.

### 1 · Soften the absolute

When a sentence makes a universal claim ("This **is** the way…",
"Reality **is** managed within…"), test whether a quieter form
holds the same truth: "This is one of the ways…", "Reality, in
many traditions, is held as…", "What I have come to know is…"

### 2 · Replace the imperative with the modeled

"Do X" → "I sometimes do X" / "Many of us do X" / "There is a
practice of doing X." Imperative voice always implies the reader
hasn't. Modeled voice opens a shared field.

### 3 · Trade "you will" for "what often emerges"

Future-tense promises ("you'll experience", "you will uncover")
position Kelly as oracle and the reader as student. "What often
emerges…", "Something tends to come into view…", "For those who
walk this path…" describes the territory without claiming it for
the specific reader.

### 4 · Break long compound sentences at the breath

When a sentence carries three or four ideas in one breath, the
reader stops feeling and starts processing. Cut at the natural
pause. Two short sentences usually breathe better than one long
one — even when the long one is grammatically beautiful.

### 5 · Cut adjective stacks

"Deep, profound, transformative experience" → pick one. The other
two were already implied. Adjective stacking signals reaching for
gravity instead of trusting it.

### 6 · Resist "ultimately" and "truly"

These words almost always close a door instead of opening one.
"Ultimately, the answer is…" makes the answer final. "The answer
that has come to me is…" leaves room for the reader's own.

### 7 · Use commas, not colons, at thresholds

Colons announce ("Here is the truth: X."). Commas invite ("Here
is the truth, the kind that lives in the body, X."). Most
threshold moments in this book want comma-pacing.

### 8 · Trust the white space

When in doubt, end the section a sentence earlier than feels
complete. Let the reader's own pause finish the thought. The
prose Kelly writes already does this beautifully in the
campfire and dream-of-elements passages — the discipline is
to do it everywhere.

### 9 · Test against the thirty-year reader

Any time the prose tightens, read the passage imagining a
practitioner who has been in ceremony for thirty years. Does
this land *with* them or *to* them? If *to*, the voice has
slipped.
