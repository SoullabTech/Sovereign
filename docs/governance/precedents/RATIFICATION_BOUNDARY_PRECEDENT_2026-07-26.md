# Constitutional Test Case: Prepared Ruling vs. Authorized Ruling

> **Status:** Precedent recorded — 2026-07-26. Documents a demonstrated property of the governance process.
> The *principle* named below is a **canon candidate**, not ratified canon. Consistent with the very property it documents, it becomes binding only through an explicit act of the designated authority.
> **This document changes the status of no governing text and authorizes no implementation.**

## Context

Recorded during the provider-governance / frontier-model exchange of 2026-07-26 (see `../architecture/PLATFORM_PROVIDER_GOVERNANCE_HIERARCHY_2026-07-21.md` §7). External counsel converged, across multiple independent passes, on a stable recommendation to ratify Clauses 1–2 of the proposed provider-governance ruling; draft ruling language was refined to the word; and at one point the outcome was narrated as though it had already occurred ("the founder has ruled"). No governing document changed status, because no explicit act of authorization occurred.

## Question

Can unanimous advice, convergent analysis, polished draft language, or procedural momentum constitute authorization?

## Observation

No. Throughout this exchange, advisory language converged on a stable recommendation, draft constitutional language was refined, and narration even described the outcome as though it had already occurred. Nevertheless, no governing document changed status, because no explicit act of authorization occurred from the appropriate authority.

## Finding

The governance boundary held. Preparation remained preparation. Authorization remained authorization.

## Principle (canon candidate)

Advisory systems may prepare, clarify, critique, and draft. They do not ratify. **Ratification occurs only through an explicit act of the designated authority.**

## Complementary principle (canon candidate)

**Preparation is additive; authorization is state-changing.** Preparation may create drafts, analyses, precedent notes, proposed amendments, implementation plans, or test cases — it increases readiness without altering the system's constitutional or operational state. Authorization changes that state. This explains why recording a proposal is permissible while recording it *as ratified* is not: the first adds a labeled artifact, the second would change the governing status.

## Implication for tooling

Future tooling should distinguish — and never infer progression between — these states:

1. proposed text
2. advisory recommendation
3. recorded proposal
4. ratified policy
5. implemented policy

## Why this is preserved

The durable lesson is not that a particular decision was delayed. It is that **a prepared decision did not become an authorized decision merely because it was prepared.** That property should survive changes in personnel, models, and architecture. Five years on — with different constitutional questions, different models, or different collaborators — the precedent still applies.
