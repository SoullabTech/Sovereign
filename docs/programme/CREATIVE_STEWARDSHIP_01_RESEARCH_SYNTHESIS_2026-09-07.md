# CREATIVE-STEWARDSHIP-01 — Research Synthesis + First Instrument

**Status:** RESEARCH · provisional · no build authorization
**Companion:** `CREATIVE_STEWARDSHIP_01_RESEARCH_CHARTER_2026-09-07.md`

## 1. The research question

Writer's Studio is for creative people. The central design question is therefore not whether an AI can complete writing tasks efficiently.

It is:

> **What conditions help a writer stay in living relationship with their own creative intelligence — and how can a digital environment, including MAIA, protect and deepen those conditions?**

The product should be evaluated as a **creativity-support environment**, not primarily as a productivity application or chatbot.

## 2. What the research already tells us

### A. Creative work is not a linear completion pipeline

Keith Sawyer's study of professional art/design pedagogy describes creative process as nonlinear, iterative and improvisational, with eight recurring characteristics: **iteration, ambiguity, exploration, emergence, failure, deliberation, reflection and constraint**.

**Writer's Studio implication:** uncertainty is not automatically a defect state. A system that always resolves, summarizes, recommends or advances may damage the very process it is trying to support.

Source: Sawyer, R. K. (2021), *The iterative and improvisational nature of the creative process*, Journal of Creativity 31, 100002. DOI: 10.1016/j.yjoc.2021.100002.

### B. Creative support has established experiential dimensions

The Creativity Support Index (CSI) evaluates a tool across six factors: **Exploration, Expressiveness, Immersion, Enjoyment, Results Worth Effort, and Collaboration**.

This is useful because it measures the experience of creating with a tool rather than trying to score whether an artifact is objectively 'creative'.

**Writer's Studio implication:** a strong launch instrument should ask whether the Studio expands exploration and expression, permits immersion, feels worth the effort and supports relationship — not whether the writer merely completed more actions.

Source: Cherry, E. & Latulipe, C. (2014), *Quantifying the Creativity Support of Digital Tools through the Creativity Support Index*, ACM TOCHI 21(4), Article 21. DOI: 10.1145/2617588.

### C. Autonomy, competence and relatedness are not soft extras

Self-Determination Theory identifies autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs whose support is associated with more volitional, persistent and creative engagement. Experience-sampling research on everyday creativity also found all three mediated the relationship between creative activity and emotional well-being.

**Writer's Studio implication:** emotionally intelligent support should strengthen:

- **autonomy** — I choose what happens to my work;
- **competence** — I feel increasingly able to work with what I am making;
- **relatedness** — I feel accompanied, understood and connected without being controlled.

Sources: Self-Determination Theory; Putney et al. (2024), Journal of Research in Personality 113, 104552.

### D. AI can reduce the felt ownership of writing even while making it easier

A 2026 preregistered creative-writing study (N=273) found manual writing produced higher psychological ownership than all AI-assisted workflows. Editing and iterative prompting increased ownership relative to more delegated workflows.

Another 2026 study found passive AI use reduced self-efficacy, ownership and work meaningfulness, while active collaboration mitigated those effects.

A systematic review of 62 human-AI co-creative systems found that high user control was associated with greater satisfaction, trust and ownership, while proactive AI worked best when adaptive and context-sensitive.

**Writer's Studio implication:** 'more capable MAIA' is not automatically a better creative experience. The design objective is **agency-preserving collaboration**.

Sources: Pennanen, Kanerva & Guckelsberger (2026), *How prompting and editing shape psychological ownership in AI-assisted creative writing*, Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans 9, 100381; PubMed 41833936; Singh et al. (2025), *A Systematic Review of Human-AI Co-Creativity*.

### E. Artistic communities notice genericity and misalignment

In qualitative work with professional comedians using LLMs, participants frequently found generated material bland, trope-heavy or poorly aligned to their artistic and community values. The study argues for artist/community-specific alignment and data ownership rather than assuming generic model helpfulness.

**Writer's Studio implication:** preserving a writer's voice is not cosmetic personalization. It is part of creative sovereignty.

Source: Mirowski et al. (2024), *A Robot Walks into a Bar: Can Language Models Serve as Creativity Support Tools for Comedy?*

## 3. Derived experience doctrine — provisional

### 3.1 Meet the state before offering the capability

The same capability can be supportive in one creative state and destructive in another.

Examples:

- In **immersion**, the intelligent act may be silence.
- In **stuckness**, one well-chosen question may be more useful than ten generated alternatives.
- In **uncertainty**, premature structure may foreclose discovery.
- In **revision**, explicit analytical help may be welcome.
- In **return**, orientation and memory may matter more than generation.

The Studio should therefore ask, implicitly or explicitly: **what kind of creative moment is this?**

### 3.2 Intervention should be permissioned by posture

MAIA may be capable of witnessing, reflecting, mapping, generating, editing and evaluating. Those are not interchangeable acts.

A shift from reflection into generation or evaluation changes the relationship to authorship. The writer should be able to invite that shift rather than discover after the fact that it happened.

### 3.3 The smallest useful intervention is often the most creative one

A creativity-support system should not demonstrate intelligence when a smaller act preserves more of the writer's own cognition.

Prefer, when sufficient:

**notice → reflect → ask → offer a door → generate only when invited**

### 3.4 Preserve productive ambiguity

Ambiguity can be generative. The Studio should distinguish:

- **confusion caused by the interface** — resolve it;
- **uncertainty inside the creative process** — do not automatically resolve it.

### 3.5 Immersion is success when the tool disappears

For creative work, 'engagement' can mean forgetting the software exists because attention is fully on the work. The Studio should not interrupt that state to promote features, insights, streaks, suggestions or MAIA herself.

### 3.6 Enchantment is not decoration

For this research, enchantment means a felt quality in which the creative act becomes more alive, resonant, spacious or meaningful.

It may arise through:

- beautiful restraint;
- language that respects the creative act;
- continuity with the writer's own imagery and voice;
- meaningful transitions and thresholds;
- surprise that reveals rather than distracts;
- a sense that the Studio remembers the living thread of the work;
- moments in which the writer sees something in their own work they had not yet seen.

A visual effect that calls attention to the product rather than the work is not automatically enchanting.

### 3.7 The return matters as much as the session

Creative work includes incubation. Leaving is not abandonment. Writer's Studio should research how a person returns after hours, days or weeks and quickly recovers:

- what they were inside;
- what felt alive;
- what remained unresolved;
- what they deliberately left alone;
- what changed in them while away.

MAIA's memory may be uniquely useful here if it restores orientation without pretending to know what the work now means.

## 4. What we should measure

Use the CSI as an established backbone, then add Writer's Studio stewardship measures.

### CSI backbone

- Exploration
- Expressiveness
- Immersion
- Enjoyment
- Results Worth Effort
- Collaboration / relational support where applicable

### Stewardship measures

After a real creative session, ask the writer to rate and discuss:

1. **I felt like the author throughout.**
2. **The Studio helped me stay close to what I was trying to make.**
3. **I discovered something in my own work.**
4. **I felt free to ignore MAIA.**
5. **MAIA intervened at the right depth.**
6. **The experience gave me more creative energy than it took.**
7. **I could remain uncertain without being pushed toward an answer.**
8. **The Studio protected my concentration.**
9. **I would want to return to this work here.**
10. **What happened still feels like mine.**

Do not optimize these into a single score yet. The language itself is under research.

## 5. Founder phenomenology — first instrument

Use a real manuscript and work naturally. Do not perform test cases. Do not try to visit every feature.

Whenever a moment has charge — positive or negative — mark it with one or two words and continue. Do not stop the creative session to analyze the product.

Suggested marks:

**alive · curious · absorbed · beautiful · surprised · recognized · accompanied · courageous · confused · pressured · interrupted · flattened · managed · lonely · dead · want-to-continue · want-to-leave**

After the session, reconstruct only the charged moments using:

```text
CREATIVE STATE
What state was I in as a writer?

MOMENT
What happened?

FELT EFFECT
What changed in me — attention, energy, courage, curiosity, ownership?

SYSTEM ACT
What did the Studio / MAIA actually do?

CREATIVE CONSEQUENCE
Did that deepen or weaken my relationship to the work?

BETTER POSSIBILITY
If this moment could meet a creative person more fully, what might it feel like instead?
```

No feature proposal is required. A moment may remain an observation.

## 6. Next research after founder walk

Do not jump directly to implementation. Use the founder walk to identify 3–5 high-charge moments, then take those to a small set of working writers/creatives.

For each moment compare possible postures, not cosmetic variants. Examples:

- silent Studio vs proactive suggestion;
- reflective question vs generated rewrite;
- orientation vs recommendation;
- one invitation vs a menu of capabilities;
- explicit MAIA presence vs background/quiet presence;
- conventional save/version language vs a more meaningful threshold that remains truthful.

The criterion is not which condition produces more clicks. It is which condition better supports **agency, immersion, expression, discovery and desire to continue**.

## 7. Current hypothesis

> Writer's Studio will feel emotionally intelligent not when it expresses more emotion, but when it recognizes the creative situation well enough to know **how much presence the writer needs, what kind, and when to get out of the way.**

And its enchantment will come not from making MAIA impressive, but from repeatedly making the writer's own work feel newly alive.
