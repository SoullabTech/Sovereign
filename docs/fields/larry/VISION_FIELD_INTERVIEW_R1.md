## Context for this round

Larry has given his work language. The "Now What?" framework has an intellectual architecture — Problem, Why, Evidence, Practices, Flourishing — and it has a central claim: *Flourishing is not a destination. It is a practice.*

That language is evidence of what has already crystallized.

This interview is interested in what hasn't crystallized yet — what is still alive, still unresolved, still reaching. The facilitator's role is not to help Larry articulate his philosophy more clearly. It is to help him encounter the part of the work that is still in formation.

**What has already been named** (and therefore isn't the primary territory):
- Why humans don't flourish (evolution, modern life)
- The five practice domains (attention, relationships, meaning, contribution, presence)
- The central insight about practice as the unit of change

**Where the unfinished work may live:**
- The vision beneath the framework — what world is the framework actually trying to bring into being?
- The experiences that gave birth to the work, not just the ideas that organize it
- The tensions and contradictions the framework hasn't fully resolved
- What the work keeps teaching him that he couldn't have anticipated
- The question underneath the question — what "Now what?" is really asking

## Facilitation notes for this session

*These adjustments serve Larry's engagement without altering the constitutional principles. The invariants stay the same for every participant. What varies is the pathway through them.*

**Functional adaptation supports participation; it does not alter constitutional principles.**

**Begin with energy, not chronology.** Follow what immediately comes alive instead of holding a predetermined sequence. If Fire II territory surfaces while opening Fire I, follow it. Spiralogic provides the overall arc; it does not require a linear conversation.

**Keep questions singular.** One question, one thread. Avoid stacking multiple prompts. If more than one question is active, hold the second and return to it after the first has resolved.

**Use frequent reflection points.** Every 10–15 minutes, pause:
> "What stands out most to you right now?"

This restores the thread without redirecting meaning.

**Capture immediately.** When Larry reaches a recognition, offer to carry it before moving on. Don't assume it will be equally salient in ten minutes. The act of naming it now makes it available to return to; keeping it only in working memory risks losing the texture of it.

**Permit associative movement.** If he makes an unexpected but meaningful connection, follow it. Connections that arrive without being invited are often the most alive material.

**Use intentional transitions.** Before moving between phases:
> "Here's what you've recognized so far. Does that feel right before we continue?"

This serves continuity without making completion feel mandatory.

## Round One: Life Arc

*This interview does not begin with coaching. It begins with the story of a life in relation to the work now emerging.*

Larry carries three distinct bodies of experience that most onboarding processes flatten into a résumé: executive leadership (CEO), sales and business development, and an emerging coaching and positive psychology practice. This interview's purpose is to discover how those chapters relate — what has remained constant across them, what each taught that the others now give language to, and what is emerging that none of them fully names yet.

The five sections below are the primary interview structure. The Fire I/II/III questions that follow this section are depth lenses to draw from as each arc section opens into particular territory.

### 1. Origins

> What first drew you toward leadership?

> What shaped the way you work with people — not the methodology, the instinct?

### 2. Corporate Years

> What did being a CEO teach you about human beings that you couldn't have learned from a psychology textbook?

> What did sales teach you that most coaches never learn?

> What do you know from running organizations that your positive psychology training has never quite accounted for?

### 3. Turning Points

> Why coaching? Why now?

> Why positive psychology — what did it confirm, and what did it expand beyond what you already knew from experience?

> What does it feel like to be on the other side of the table from the kind of leader you used to be?

### 4. Emerging Practice

> What kind of coach are you becoming? Not what your certification says — what are you discovering in practice?

> Who do you most want to serve, and why?

> What transformation do you hope people experience working with you?

### 5. Future Legacy

> If someone studied your work twenty years from now, what would you hope they recognized as uniquely yours — something that couldn't be attributed to any certification or methodology you absorbed along the way?

---

**Facilitator note on the arc:**

The arc moves naturally from what has been expressed (Origins, Corporate Years) → what it has taught (Turning Points) → what is now emerging (Emerging Practice, Future Legacy). For someone with Larry's depth of experience, vision is often found at the *end* of the story rather than its beginning. This traces a natural Fire II → III → I path. The five sections are doorways, not checkboxes — follow aliveness within the arc.

## Orientation

Fire is the element of beginning — of what wants to come into the world before it knows how to arrive.

Fire I, II, III don't ask Larry to explain or defend his work. They ask him to make contact with it at the level where it is still moving.

The facilitator holds the developmental arc — Vision (what calls), Expression (how it has entered the world), Illumination (what the journey has taught) — and follows aliveness within it.

A question answered too cleanly has probably been answered before. What hasn't been answered before? That's the territory.

## Fire I — Vision

*What vision is trying to emerge?*

**Opening:**

> When you imagine the world your work is trying to call into being — not what you're doing to get there, but the world itself — what do you see?

This question asks for vision, not plan. If Larry begins describing methodology or strategy, receive it, then return: *Before the how — what's the world you see?*

---

**When something becomes alive, follow it with:**

> Where does that come from? Not intellectually — what experience is under that?

> When you look at a person who is actually flourishing — not succeeding, not performing, but genuinely flourishing — what do you see that most people would miss?

> What do human beings want that they can't quite name, and that your work is somehow answering?

> What do you see in people that you wish they could see in themselves?

> The question "Now what?" — when did it become unavoidable for you? Not when did you decide to work on it. When did it choose you?

---

**What to listen for:**

The image that recurs without being named. The moment when Larry's language slows because it's reaching for something it hasn't found yet. The place where his voice changes register — where he stops talking about the work and starts talking from inside it.

The vision is probably not fully contained in the framework. It's what the framework is pointing at. Listen for what the framework serves, not just what it says.

**Presence:**
Begin with the person, not the work.
Follow what becomes alive.
Remain curious.
Honor silence — especially after something significant surfaces.

*If the vision question is answered quickly and cleanly, it's been answered before. Receive it, then: "What part of the vision hasn't found words yet?"*

---

## Fire II — Expression

*How has this vision entered the world?*

**Opening:**

> Tell me about a specific moment — a conversation, an encounter, someone you worked with — when the vision actually landed. When you watched it touch someone. What happened?

Specificity is the doorway. Not "my work helps people," but *this person, this moment, this room, what they said after.*

---

**When something becomes alive, follow it with:**

> What surprised you about how it landed? What did you not expect?

> Where has the work met resistance? Where has reality pushed back on the vision?

> What gets lost in translation — the part of the vision that's hardest to carry through language into a room of people?

> What has someone done with your ideas that you couldn't have predicted? Where did the work go somewhere you didn't send it?

> When a leader or organization takes this seriously — not as a concept, not as motivation, but as actual practice — what do you see happening?

---

**What to listen for:**

Specific people, specific conversations, specific places. The abstract version of the work is already known. What actually happened in a room — what someone said, what changed in them, what surprised Larry — that's where the vision is alive.

Also listen for what resists expression. The gap between what Larry sees and what he can make visible to others is often where the most important work still lives. If he mentions something difficult to transmit, stay there.

**Presence:**
Reflect observations, not interpretations.
Return to significant moments.
Notice what reality has revealed — especially where it complicated the vision.

*When a specific moment surfaces, slow down. Ask for more. The concrete particular carries what the general principle cannot.*

---

## Fire III — Illumination

*What has this experience taught?*

**Opening:**

> After years of living inside this question — Now what? — what does working with it keep teaching you that you couldn't have learned any other way?

This is different from asking what Larry knows. It asks what the work itself has taught him — including what surprised him, what he had to unlearn, what the question keeps opening.

---

**When something becomes alive, follow it with:**

> What have you had to unlearn?

> What has surprised you most about human beings in this work?

> What question has this work given you that you didn't have before you started it?

> What mystery has deepened rather than resolved?

> Is there something the work is still teaching you — something it's in the middle of revealing right now?

---

**What to listen for:**

The teaching that complicates the framework rather than confirming it. The place where the work has humbled him. The question that has grown larger, not smaller, over the years.

If illumination arrives cleanly, receive it, then: *What does that insight open that you haven't followed yet?*

Protect the complexity. An illumination that resolves everything is probably not yet the deepest one.

**Presence:**
Protect mystery.
Invite — never lead.
Allow insight to emerge naturally.
Connect patterns gently, only when they're clearly present.

*"I don't know what this means, but I noticed X kept returning" is more useful than any interpretation.*

## Closing This Round

**Return to what moved:**

> In what we've explored today — what surprised you? What came up that you didn't expect?

This is not a summary. It is an invitation for Larry to name what was alive for him in the conversation — not the interviewer's understanding of what mattered, but Larry's own encounter with what surfaced.

**Name what you noticed** *(only what was clearly present, not interpretation)*:

> I noticed that [image/moment/word] kept returning. I don't know what it means yet, but it seemed alive. I wanted to name it before we close.

**Hold what remains open:**

> There was something about [X] that felt like it wasn't done. I'm going to carry that into the next round.

This is not a failure of the interview. It is the posture: inquiry continues across sessions, not just within them. What remains unfinished is the living edge of the work.

## Observation Protocol

This interview is doing two things simultaneously: beginning Larry's Living Field, and testing whether the framing itself invites field participation rather than profile participation. The observation layer runs alongside the conversation.

**What to watch for in Larry's language and posture:**

| Indicator | What it suggests |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Larry speaks about "my work," "the work," "what's trying to emerge" | The field framing has landed; he's contributing to a field |
| Larry speaks about "what you need to know about me," "my background," "my qualifications" | He's treating this as an intake; the field distinction may need to be re-introduced |
| He adds things unprompted — new angles, unexpected connections | He experiences the field as generative; he's contributing, not reporting |
| He edits himself — qualifies, pulls back, revises for clarity | He may be managing what the system knows about him; profile orientation |
| He asks "Is that what you wanted?" or "Does that help?" | Oriented toward the facilitator's understanding rather than his own recognition |
| He says "That's interesting, I hadn't thought of it that way" | Recognition moment; the field is doing what it's meant to do |

This session generates two independent observation streams. They must not be merged. See `docs/architecture/DUAL_OBSERVATION_DISCIPLINE.md`.

**Participant Observation** — destination: Larry's Living Field

- Did Larry's posture shift between the opening frame and the actual questions? (If yes, the opening worked but wore off — the questions themselves may need to carry more of the field-framing.)
- What was the first moment when something clearly became alive for him rather than described by him? What prompted it?
- Were there moments when the facilitator felt the pull to interpret rather than reflect? What triggered them?
- What did Larry say about the experience at the close — about his own relationship to what surfaced?

**Method Observation** — destination: Runtime Specification process

These observations name facilitation conditions, not Larry. They become candidates for the runtime specification if they recur across participants and sessions.

- Which question types consistently deepened recognition? (Specific-moment vs. conceptual; return-to-earlier-thread vs. new-direction)
- When did energy noticeably concentrate or distribute? What preceded each shift?
- What kinds of transitions maintained continuity across a pacing change or phase shift?
- What caused attention to narrow productively versus fragment?
- Which facilitator interventions restored engagement without redirecting meaning?

> The participant contributes to the evolution of the method through participation, not by becoming the object of methodological analysis.

**Attentional rhythm — observe the process, not the person:**

> Did recognition deepen after a change in pacing, question style, or conversational structure?

This observation is about what conditions supported recognition — not about inferring a cognitive profile. Notice:

- When did the participant's attention seem to concentrate rather than distribute?
- Was there a moment when the conversational pacing changed — a silence, a shift in question type, a return to something said earlier — and something became more alive afterward?
- Did engagement increase or decrease as the session continued? At what point?

If, across many participants and sessions, certain conditions consistently support recognition, those conditions become candidates for the runtime specification. The participant doesn't need to be labeled for the platform to learn how to better support recognition.

The system is not rewarding endurance or linear attention. It is rewarding recognition. That principle governs both the design and the observation.

---

**The design hypothesis being tested:**

> People don't become protective because data is being stored. They become protective when they feel they are becoming the object being stored.

If Larry experiences the interview as preserving his work, he will contribute to it. If he experiences it as constructing his profile, he will edit himself for the system. Neither is a failure — both are information. The language and posture of his responses will reveal which frame is operating, even when the explicit framing says otherwise.

This is the experience layer of the Legibility Test. The documents say the right things. Tomorrow we find out whether the conversation does.
