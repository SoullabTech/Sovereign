# What great human editors and writing mentors do with writers: practices an AI companion could embody

Scope note: these notes cover developmental editors, writing teachers and writing-center tutors. Most sources reached in this pass are secondary: summaries, interviews, teaching blogs and reviews. Where a quotation is secondhand, it is marked. Two PDFs (Sommers 1982 at uky.edu and Lerman's one-page CRP brief) were fetched, but their text could not be extracted (compressed PDF streams), so claims about them rest on secondary summaries. Product constraints applied throughout: the companion does not write the writer's prose unless asked, authority stays with the writer, and the writer is never ranked or graded.

## 1. Developmental editing practice: what an editor does first, how they read, how they give feedback, and how they protect the author's voice

### Takeaway
The great developmental editors start by reading for the whole: surrendering to the book, then responding first with what is alive in it. Their feedback is mostly about one large structural or character issue, not the sentences. Their working creed is that the book belongs to the author. The cautionary case (Lish and Carver) shows what happens when an editor's taste replaces the author's voice: the work may succeed while the author experiences "amputation."

### Cited Findings
- **Perkins's credo.** A. Scott Berg reports that Perkins often stated his credo, "The book belongs to the author," and that he stayed faithful to it even while doing heavy manuscript work. — [Library of America blog quoting Berg](https://blog.loa.org/2010/09/maxwell-perkins-editor-of-f-scott.html); [National Book Review Q&A with Berg](https://www.thenationalbookreview.com/features/2016/6/14/qa-a-scott-berg-on-his-max-perkins-biography-now-a-big-movie-starring-jude-law). The results give no primary letter or page cite for the wording.
- **Berg on overreach.** Berg concedes the strongest argument that Perkins overreached is his editing of Thomas Wolfe (*Of Time and the River*), although Berg himself argues he did not. — [National Book Review Q&A](https://www.thenationalbookreview.com/features/2016/6/14/qa-a-scott-berg-on-his-max-perkins-biography-now-a-big-movie-starring-jude-law)
- **Perkins's first response to *Gatsby*.** It was wholehearted, specific appreciation before any critique: "I think the novel is a wonder. I'm taking it home to read again and shall then write my impressions in full; — but it has vitality to an extraordinary degree, and glamour, and a great deal of underlying thought of unusual quality." It answered Fitzgerald's stated insecurity ("how good 'my own' is remains to be seen"). — [Flavorwire, via Letters of Note](https://www.flavorwire.com/305610/read-fitzgeralds-editors-first-impression-of-the-great-gatsby)
- **Perkins's suggestions.** His later suggestions concerned nearly all one thing, the character of Gatsby: readers needed a clearer sense of Jay Gatsby himself. Susan Bell's *The Artful Edit* treats this collaboration as an exemplary case study. — [Bell essay excerpt, Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/51137-guest-essay-revisioning-the-great-gatsby-iv?tab=author); [Poets & Writers listing for *The Artful Edit*](https://www.pw.org/best-books/the_artful_edit_on_the_practice_of_editing_yourself)
- **Gottlieb on reading.** In the Paris Review interview (secondhand quote), Gottlieb describes reading as a need to "surrender" to the book: "The more you have surrendered, the more jarring its errors appear." — [Paris Review, Art of Editing No. 1](https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/1760/the-art-of-editing-no-1-robert-gottlieb), as quoted in [Ragan](https://ragan.com/?p=311528) and [The Rumpus](https://therumpus.net/2011/05/03/spotlighting-the-editor/)
- **Gottlieb on invisibility.** He held that "The editor's relationship to a book should be an invisible one," and that "This glorification of editors, of which I have been an extreme example, is not a wholesome thing." Both quotes are secondhand. — [The Rumpus](https://therumpus.net/2011/05/03/spotlighting-the-editor/); [Ragan](https://ragan.com/?p=311528)
- **Morrison on her job.** She described her role with Toni Cade Bambara narrowly: "My only job with Bambara was to make sure that the story held together." Bambara would write upstairs, come down for editing advice, then go back up to make the changes herself, so the author did the rewriting. — [Lit Hub, "7 Writers Who Were Also Editors"](https://lithub.com/7-writers-who-were-also-editors-and-the-books-they-edited); [Nieman Storyboard on Dana Williams, *Toni at Random*](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=15812)
- **Morrison's other practices.** She accompanied the shy Gayl Jones to interviews, which was care for the writer as a person and not only the text. She also pushed writers to resist the standard approach to writing about people of color. — [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=15812); [Literary Ladies Guide](https://www.literaryladiesguide.com/?p=26290)
- **Lerner on ambivalence.** *The Forest for the Trees* centres the "ambivalent writer," who oscillates between "I am great" and "I am shit." The writers who finish are the ones who hold that ambivalence long enough to commit. Reviewers note she is attuned to writers' need for encouragement. — [Christina Baker Kline on Lerner](https://christinabakerkline.com/tag/ambivalence); [Writers' Services review](https://writersservices.co.uk/content/forest-trees-reviews); [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781573221528)
- **Lish and Carver (cautionary case).** Lish reportedly cut about half of Carver's words and rewrote ten of thirteen stories in *What We Talk About When We Talk About Love*. On July 8, 1980, Carver wrote begging Lish to halt production and called the cuts "surgical amputation and transplant." The book was published with Lish's edits anyway. The unedited versions later appeared as *Beginners*. — [NYRB, "Two Raymond Carvers"](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2010/05/27/two-raymond-carvers/); [Ploughshares, "Carver, Lish, and the Editor as Enabler"](https://pshares.org/?p=49224); [The Millions](https://themillions.com/?p=8867). Sources differ on the magnitude: rumours of 70% cuts versus about 50%.
- **Le Guin on workshops.** Her rule (secondhand quote) is that the author stays silent before and during critique, and she calls this essential rather than arbitrary. She also says, "Ultimately you write alone… you and you alone can judge your work," and warns that workshops can make writers dependent on others' opinions. — [The Archetypist on *Steering the Craft*](https://thearchetypist.substack.com/p/art-and-craft-steering-the-craft); [Goodreads, Le Guin "no recipes"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/16261141-ursula-k-le-guin-on-writing-alas-there-are-no-recipes)

### Inferences
- **Observable editor sequence:** (1) read the whole work first, without intervening (surrender); (2) give an honest, specific response to what is alive, tied to the writer's own stated hope or fear (Perkins's "wonder" letter); (3) reread; (4) raise the one load-bearing issue, usually character, structure or coherence, and frame it as what readers need; (5) the author does the rewriting.
- **"The book belongs to the author," Gottlieb's invisibility and Morrison's "only job" all map directly onto the no-prose-unasked constraint.** The companion's job is to help the story "hold together" on the writer's terms.
- **Lish and Carver is the defining anti-pattern for an AI companion.** Rewriting toward the editor's aesthetic (here, minimalism) can produce admired work that the author experiences as amputation. An AI that "improves" prose by default risks exactly this at scale.
- **Lerner's ambivalence frame suggests presence includes steadiness:** neither feeding the "I am shit" swing nor the "I am great" one, but helping the writer commit to their own vision.
- **Le Guin's "you alone can judge your work" supports keeping evaluation with the writer.** It also warns against fostering dependence on the companion's opinion.

### Gaps
- Bell's own wording on Perkins's methods (editorial letter structure, margin notes) was not retrieved. The book or her Tin House essay is needed.
- I found no primary-source text of Perkins's full *Gatsby* critique letter (Letters of Note link not fetched).
- The Gottlieb quotes are secondhand. The Paris Review page itself was not read.
- Lerner's specific advice on how editors deliver feedback was not retrieved. The sources cover her stance on writers' psychology only.
- Morrison's own editorial philosophy is sparsely documented; the main source is Williams's *Toni at Random*.

## 2. Writing pedagogy: Murray, Elbow, writing centres (North, Brooks, Harris), Sommers, Lerman CRP, Amherst Writers (Schneider)

### Takeaway
Across composition studies the consensus is: respond to the writer rather than correct the text, listen more than talk, and describe the reader's experience rather than issue verdicts. Ask questions that let the writer find their own answers, and never take over the paper or appropriate its purpose. Each tradition encodes the writer's authority structurally: who speaks first, who holds the pen, and when opinions are allowed.

### Cited Findings
- **Murray's conference method.** Developed at the University of New Hampshire, it advised teachers to "be quiet, to listen, to respond" ("The Listening Eye," 1979). His reasoning: "When [the teacher is] talking [the student] isn't writing." — [Wikipedia, Donald Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Murray_(writer)); [Murray, "Teach Writing as a Process Not Product" (PDF)](https://oira.aua.am/files/2020/02/Teach-Writing-as-a-Process-Not-Product.pdf)
- **Who leads in conferences.** Harris, describing conference pedagogy, notes that the writer leads and the teacher follows. She also cites warnings against conferences where teachers merely issue directions. — [Harris, *Teaching One-to-One*, ch. 2 (WAC Clearinghouse)](https://innovationtest1.colostate.edu/books/harris/chapter2.pdf)
- **Harris on conference activities.** The core activities are listen, ask questions, observe, demonstrate strategies, and respond. Listening "is more a deliberate act than a natural one" (citing Donald Graves). Tutors listen both for answers and "to hear writers talking about their writing and raising questions of their own." — [Harris, *Teaching One-to-One*](https://innovationtest1.colostate.edu/books/harris/chapter3.pdf), as summarized in search excerpts
- **Harris on non-judgemental space.** She argues tutors must build a non-judgemental setting "where there is no penalty for trying out new ideas." In "Talking in the Middle," she shows that tutor questions help writers surface information they did not know was needed. — [JALT TLT summary](https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2335-conferencing-interactive-way-teach-writing); [Harris, "Talking in the Middle" (PDF)](https://qcwc.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/4142/files/2018/09/HARRIS_talking_the_middle_why_writers_need_writing_tutors.pdf)
- **North's dictum.** Stephen North's "The Idea of a Writing Center" (*College English*, 1984, p. 438) states that the job is to produce "better writers, not better writing." The aim is to change writers, not necessarily their texts. — [WAC Clearinghouse chapter](https://wacclearinghouse.org/docs/books/usu/location/chapter6.pdf)
- **Critiques of North.** Latta notes "better" is undefined and asks: better for whom? He also warns the approach may just shift "repair" from grammar to the person. — [WLN 44.3, Latta](https://wac.colostate.edu/docs/wln/v44n3/latta.pdf)
- **Brooks, "Minimalist Tutoring: Making the Student Do All the Work"** (*Writing Lab Newsletter*, 1991). A tutor who fixes the paper becomes an editor, not a tutor. Tutors should instead be "interested readers." Basic practices: sit beside the writer; keep the paper closer to the writer than to the tutor; do not write on the paper; have the writer read aloud. Advanced practices: point out strengths, ask questions that lead to understanding, and set the writer a small task while the tutor steps away. — [Purdue Global Writing Center summary](https://purdueglobalwriting.center/2015/01/07/minimalist-tutoringonline/)
- **Elbow's "movies of the reader's mind."** "To improve your writing you don't need advice about what changes to make; you don't need theories of what is good and bad writing. You need movies of people's minds while they read your words" (*Writing Without Teachers*, p. 77). A movie is "a subjective but factual statement of what happens when you read… reactions and not just conclusions." — [Lawyer as Writer, Elbow summary](https://lawlit.net/writeshop/writeshop/movie.html)
- **Elbow's four ways to give a "movie":** pointing, summarizing, telling, showing. Pointing means naming the words that "most successfully penetrated your skull… rang true," and also the ones that "ring false, hollow, plastic" (pp. 85–86). — [Lawyer as Writer](https://lawlit.net/writeshop/writeshop/movie.html)
- **Elbow's two kinds of feedback.** In *Writing With Power* (pp. 240–255) he distinguishes **criterion-based** feedback (quality of content, organization, language, usage, i.e., diagnosis of strengths and weaknesses) from **reader-based** feedback, which "tells you what it [your writing] does to readers." — [Lawyer as Writer](https://lawlit.net/writeshop/writeshop/movie.html)
- **Sommers, "Responding to Student Writing"** (*CCC* 33.2, 1982). She studied 35 teachers' comments on the same drafts. Comments were often generic and "rubber-stamped," interchangeable across texts. Teachers **appropriated** student texts by substituting their own purposes. Sentence-level comments distracted from purpose and argument. — [ECU WAC summary](https://writing.ecu.edu/wac/responding-to-student-writing/); [secondary summary blog](https://epistemicexigence.blogspot.com/2015/09/nancy-sommers-responding-to-student.html); full text at [UKY (PDF)](https://wrd.as.uky.edu/sites/default/files/Sommers.Responding-to-Student.1982.pdf) (not machine-readable in this pass)
- **Lerman's Critical Response Process** (1990) has three roles: artist, responders and facilitator. The steps: (1) **Statements of Meaning**, where responders say what was meaningful, evocative or striking; (2) **Artist as Questioner**, where the maker asks their own questions first; (3) **Neutral Questions** from responders, open and without embedded opinion; (4) opinions, given only with the artist's permission. — [Purdue OWL on CRP](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/creative_writing/teachers/workshop_creative_writing_guide/liz_lerman_critical_response_process.html); [Liz Lerman, CRP basics](https://lizlerman.com/2020/04/24/critical-response-process-basics). Note: the search summary confirmed steps 1–3. The name of step 4 ("Permissioned Opinions") and its "I have an opinion about X; would you like to hear it?" form come from my background knowledge and should be checked against Lerman and Borstel's book.
- **Amherst Writers & Artists (Pat Schneider, from 1979).** The method honours each writer's "primary voice." It rests on Five Essential Affirmations, including that every person has a strong, unique voice and that writing is an art form available to all. Its practice puts "praise for what is strong first in every response." Confidentiality is foundational: what is shared stays in the group. — [Wikipedia, Pat Schneider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Schneider); [Writing Ourselves Whole, AWA method](https://writingourselveswhole.org/about/awa-method/); [Schneider blog via Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author/show/205051.Pat_Schneider/blog)

### Inferences
- **Every tradition structurally protects the writer's authority, which is directly implementable:**
  - CRP: the artist's questions come before responders' opinions, and opinions require permission.
  - Brooks: the writer keeps the pen and the paper sits closer to them.
  - Le Guin and Murray: the guide talks less.
  - AWA: what is strong comes first.
  - Elbow: report the reading experience, not a verdict.
- **Elbow's reader-based feedback is the cleanest fit for the no-ranking constraint.** "Here is what happened in me as I read paragraph 3" describes an effect without grading the writer. Criterion-based diagnosis is closer to grading and should be optional or invited.
- **Sommers's "appropriation" and "rubber-stamped" findings define two failure modes for an AI:**
  - Imposing its own purpose on the text.
  - Generic comments that could attach to any text. Specificity to *this* text is the antidote.
- **North's critics (Latta) warn that "better writers" can itself become a project done to the person.** An AI should avoid treating the writer as something to fix, which is consistent with no grading.

### Gaps
- Elbow's "believing game / doubting game" (from *Writing Without Teachers*' appendix essay, later expanded) was not sourced in this pass. My background understanding is that the believing game means entering an idea fully to see what it gets right before doubting it. That needs a citation before use.
- *Writing Without Teachers*' "teacherless class" protocol was not retrieved.
- The Sommers primary text was not extracted, so exact quotations (e.g., "appropriate," "rubber-stamped") are via secondary sources.
- The AWA practice of responding to the writing as fiction (not assuming the narrator is the writer) was searched for but not confirmed.
- Brannon and Knoblauch, "On Students' Rights to Their Own Texts" (an adjacent appropriation argument), was not retrieved; its date conflicts across sources (1982 vs 1984).

## 3. The sequence of a good editorial conversation: when a mentor asks versus tells, and how they choose one thing

### Takeaway
The sequence that recurs across sources is:

1. Read the whole.
2. Say what is alive and meaningful.
3. Let the writer say what *they* want to know.
4. Ask neutral, open questions.
5. Offer a reader's-mind account.
6. Offer an opinion or suggestion only with permission, and then about the single largest issue.

Asking dominates early; telling is late, invited and narrow.

### Cited Findings
- **CRP's order is explicitly a sequence:** meaning first, then the artist's questions, then neutral questions, and only then permissioned opinion. — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/creative_writing/teachers/workshop_creative_writing_guide/liz_lerman_critical_response_process.html); [Lerman](https://lizlerman.com/2020/04/24/critical-response-process-basics)
- **AWA rule:** praise for what is strong comes first in *every* response, "giving the emerging poet a foundation of strength on which to offer suggestions." — [Schneider blog via Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author/show/205051.Pat_Schneider/blog)
- **Brooks's "advanced" moves follow the same order:** point out strengths, then ask questions that lead the writer to understanding, then step away while the writer works. — [Purdue Global](https://purdueglobalwriting.center/2015/01/07/minimalist-tutoringonline/)
- **Perkins's letter pattern:** first an enthusiastic, specific response ("a wonder… vitality… glamour"), then a reread, then concentrated suggestions on essentially one issue, the character of Gatsby. — [Flavorwire](https://www.flavorwire.com/305610/read-fitzgeralds-editors-first-impression-of-the-great-gatsby); [Bell essay excerpt](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/51137-guest-essay-revisioning-the-great-gatsby-iv?tab=author)
- **Morrison's single question:** does the story hold together. — [Lit Hub](https://lithub.com/7-writers-who-were-also-editors-and-the-books-they-edited)
- **Sommers on prioritizing:** sentence-level comments distract from larger questions of purpose, argument and organization, which argues for raising the global issue rather than many local ones. — [ECU WAC](https://writing.ecu.edu/wac/responding-to-student-writing/)
- **Harris on following:** tutors use the writer's own responses as cues for the next question, so the writer leads and the tutor follows. — [Harris, "Talking in the Middle"](https://qcwc.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/4142/files/2018/09/HARRIS_talking_the_middle_why_writers_need_writing_tutors.pdf); [Harris ch. 2](https://innovationtest1.colostate.edu/books/harris/chapter2.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Ask versus tell.** Ask when the writer's intent is unknown, which is most of the time early on, since telling then risks Sommers-style appropriation. Tell only (a) information the writer explicitly requests, or (b) a permissioned opinion. Even then, frame it as a reader's experience (Elbow) or as what the story needs to hold together (Morrison), not as a rule.
- **Choosing ONE thing.** The editors converge on the highest-level issue that most affects the whole: Gatsby's character, story coherence. Sommers's evidence that local comments crowd out global ones supports deferring line-level notes. A practical heuristic: the one thing is the issue whose resolution would change the most of what the writer is trying to do, as the writer has stated it.
- **Candidate conversational sequence for the AI:**
  1. Acknowledge that it has read the actual passage.
  2. Name one specific thing that "got through" (Elbow's pointing).
  3. Ask what the writer wants to look at.
  4. Ask one neutral question.
  5. Offer at most one observation, framed as a reader's experience, and only if invited.
  6. Stop, and let the writer work (Brooks's "step away").

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study, in this pass, comparing outcomes of "one thing" versus "many things" feedback in creative-writing mentoring. The one-thing heuristic is inferred from practitioner accounts.
- I did not retrieve how editorial letters are typically structured (e.g., length, ordering conventions) from a primary editor's guide.

## 4. What creates a felt sense of presence and of being met

### Takeaway
Writers feel accompanied when the guide demonstrably attends to *their* text, responds to the specific words and to the writer's own stated hopes and fears, listens deliberately and talks less, remembers the writer as a person, and gives silence and space for the writer to work. Generic, interchangeable response destroys presence.

### Cited Findings
- **Specificity versus genericity.** Sommers found teacher comments were generic and interchangeable, "rubber-stamped" from text to text, the opposite of being met. — [ECU WAC](https://writing.ecu.edu/wac/responding-to-student-writing/); [summary blog](https://epistemicexigence.blogspot.com/2015/09/nancy-sommers-responding-to-student.html)
- **Attending to the actual words.** Elbow's "pointing" asks the reader to name the exact words that "penetrated your skull." A movie of the mind relates what happened in *this* reader during *this* reading. — [Lawyer as Writer](https://lawlit.net/writeshop/writeshop/movie.html)
- **Deliberate listening.** Harris (citing Graves): listening is "more a deliberate act than a natural one." Murray: "be quiet, … listen, … respond." — [Harris summary](https://innovationtest1.colostate.edu/books/harris/chapter3.pdf); [Wikipedia, Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Murray_(writer))
- **Answering the writer's fear.** Perkins's "wonder" letter answered Fitzgerald's stated doubt about whether "my own" was good. — [Flavorwire](https://www.flavorwire.com/305610/read-fitzgeralds-editors-first-impression-of-the-great-gatsby)
- **Care for the person.** Morrison accompanied Gayl Jones to interviews when Jones was shy. — [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=15812)
- **Physical stance.** Brooks: sit beside the writer, not across; keep the paper nearer the writer; step away while they work, which gives them space. — [Purdue Global](https://purdueglobalwriting.center/2015/01/07/minimalist-tutoringonline/)
- **Safety and confidentiality as preconditions.** AWA groups begin with confidentiality, and Harris requires a setting with "no penalty for trying out new ideas." — [Writing Ourselves Whole](https://writingourselveswhole.org/about/awa-method/); [JALT TLT](https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2335-conferencing-interactive-way-teach-writing)
- **Holding the writer's ambivalence.** Lerner's book is read as a "survival course" for writers who feel alone with their work, attentive to the swings of self-doubt. — [Writers' Services review](https://writersservices.co.uk/content/forest-trees-reviews); [Christina Baker Kline](https://christinabakerkline.com/tag/ambivalence)

### Inferences
- **Observable presence behaviours for an AI companion:**
  1. Quote or point to exact phrases from the writer's text, never generic praise.
  2. Refer back to what the writer said they were trying to do, or what they feared.
  3. Ask fewer, better questions and leave room; do not fill silence with suggestions.
  4. Remember prior sessions' stated intentions, which is analogous to an editor rereading and to Morrison's continuity of relationship. This must be consent-governed in this product.
  5. Let the writer hold the "pen": offer to step back while they revise.
  6. Respond to the person's state (ambivalence, shyness) without evaluating them.
- **Test for "rubber-stamping":** could this comment be pasted onto a different manuscript? If yes, it fails presence.

### Gaps
- I found no sourced research on timing (e.g., response latency or the cadence of check-ins) in human mentoring relationships.
- No retrieved source directly treats "silence" as a deliberate mentoring tool beyond Murray's "be quiet" and Le Guin's author-silence rule (the latter concerns the *author's* silence in workshop, not the mentor's).
- Remembering as a presence factor is inferred from editorial relationships (Perkins, Morrison), not from a study.
