# Relational Consciousness

## What the Literature Establishes, What It Does Not, and Where the Real Question Begins

### Abstract

The growing language of relationality across consciousness science, social neuroscience, enactivism, psychology, psychoanalysis, and artificial-intelligence research creates both an opportunity and a conceptual hazard. "Relational" can refer to relations among experiences, relations among mental states, relations between organisms and environments, relations between persons, or interactions that help constitute cognition, selfhood, or meaning. These uses are not interchangeable. A literature showing that social interaction constitutes social cognition does not thereby show that consciousness is constituted between persons; a theory describing consciousness through relations among experiences is not necessarily interpersonal; and evidence that two interacting brains exhibit dynamics unavailable to single-brain observation does not establish a shared phenomenal subject.

This paper proposes a framework for distinguishing these claims. Drawing especially on De Jaegher, Di Paolo, and Gallagher's distinction among contextual, enabling, and constitutive relations, it argues that every relational-consciousness claim must specify two things: **the kind of relation under discussion and the phenomenon, X, that the relation is alleged to contextualize, enable, or constitute**. Applying this discipline to second-person neuroscience, dialogical theories of self, relational psychoanalysis, category-theoretic approaches to consciousness, social-neuroscientific models of awareness, and contemporary theories of artificial consciousness reveals a striking result. A substantial literature establishes that relation can be constitutive of social cognition, self-organization, interpersonal meaning, and some forms of subjective organization. What has not yet been established is the stronger proposition that phenomenal consciousness itself is constituted by the relation between distinct agents.

The resulting gap should not be treated as evidence against relational consciousness. It marks the location of the actual question.

**Keywords:** consciousness, relational consciousness, social cognition, second-person neuroscience, enactivism, dialogical self, intersubjectivity, artificial consciousness, relational ontology

---

## 1. The Problem Hidden Inside the Word "Relational"

Consciousness research traditionally asks what must occur **within** a conscious system. Competing answers differ radically. Computational functionalist approaches ask whether the relevant functional or computational organization is present. Biological-naturalist approaches question whether computation alone can be sufficient and emphasize the biological processes characteristic of living organisms. Recent work on artificial consciousness makes this disagreement especially visible. Butlin and colleagues propose theory-derived indicators that can alter our credence that an artificial system is conscious, while Anil Seth argues that consciousness may depend far more deeply on being a living biological organism than computational functionalism allows.

Yet despite their differences, both approaches tend to preserve a common geometry. They ask what must be true **of the system**.

The relevant computation is in the system.
The relevant biological organization is in the organism.
The mechanisms or properties that might support consciousness are sought primarily within the candidate conscious entity.

A relational account raises a different possibility. Perhaps some phenomena cannot be understood solely by inspecting an isolated system because something relevant arises through the system's participation in a field of interaction.

That possibility immediately encounters a problem: the literature already uses *relational* to mean several importantly different things.

Tsuchiya and Saigo, for example, explicitly describe a "relational approach to consciousness," but their relations are primarily **relations among conscious experiences**. Category theory is used to characterize an experience by the structure of its relations to other experiences. Their proposal is therefore genuinely relational, but not principally interpersonal. It does not claim that consciousness arises between persons.

Hermans' dialogical-self theory is relational in another sense. The self is decentralized and understood as a multiplicity of positions capable of entering dialogical relations, including socially and culturally mediated positions. Here relationality concerns the organization of **selfhood**.

Second-person neuroscience introduces yet another use. Conscious agents participate in real-time reciprocal interaction, and some neural processes during interaction differ from those found when a participant merely observes another person.

These literatures cannot simply be arranged along one continuum called "relational consciousness." They are changing both the meaning of *relation* and the phenomenon being explained.

That distinction is the starting point of this paper.

---

## 2. The Most Important Question: What Is X?

A particularly useful conceptual tool comes from De Jaegher, Di Paolo, and Gallagher's account of social interaction. They distinguish several explanatory roles interaction may play. Interaction can be merely **contextual** to some phenomenon, it can be **enabling**, or it can in some cases be **constitutive** of social cognition itself. Their central argument is that social interaction need not merely provide input to otherwise autonomous individual cognitive machinery; interactive processes can sometimes form part of the processes producing social cognition.

The distinction is powerful, but only if one retains its target.

A relation is not constitutive *in general*. It is constitutive **of something**.

Formally:

> **Relation R may be contextual to X, enabling of X, or constitutive of X.**

The variable **X** cannot disappear.

Suppose interaction is shown to constitute social cognition. It does not follow that interaction constitutes consciousness.

Suppose interpersonal relationships participate in constituting a dialogical self. It does not follow that they constitute phenomenality.

Suppose two brains display interactional dynamics unavailable from studying either separately. It does not follow that a third phenomenal subject exists between them.

This simple requirement—to name X—is an unusually strong protection against conceptual drift.

It also reveals why the relational-consciousness problem has remained difficult to formulate. Several neighboring literatures establish constitutive relationality, but their dependent variable changes.

| Literature                       | Relation                           | Candidate X                                      |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Second-person neuroscience       | person ↔ person                    | interactive behavior / neural dynamics           |
| Participatory sense-making       | agent ↔ agent                      | social cognition / meaning                       |
| Dialogical-self theory           | social position ↔ social position  | selfhood                                         |
| Relational psychoanalysis        | analyst ↔ analysand                | relational unconscious / subjective organization |
| Category-theoretic consciousness | experience ↔ experience            | structure/content of experience                  |
| Higher-order theories            | mental state ↔ mental state        | conscious status of a state                      |
| Social models of awareness       | self-model ↔ other-model machinery | awareness attribution                            |
| Strong relational consciousness  | agent ↔ agent                      | phenomenal consciousness itself                  |

The final row cannot be inferred merely by accumulating the rows above it.

## 3. Interaction Is Not Observation

Second-person neuroscience provides some of the strongest empirical evidence that interaction deserves to be treated as a phenomenon in its own right.

Redcay and Schilbach distinguish conventional social neuroscience—where a participant observes socially meaningful material—from a genuinely interactive second-person approach involving real-time reciprocal social interaction. Research reviewed by them indicates that neural mechanisms engaged during actual interaction can differ from those involved in social observation.

Liu and Pelowski make the methodological issue even sharper. Two-person neuroscience and hyperscanning can reveal relationships between neural activity across interacting participants, including forms of inter-brain synchronization associated with different forms of cooperation and competition. Their analysis emphasizes that different interaction structures produce different neural patterns and therefore must be distinguished experimentally.

This is important because it defeats a simplistic methodological individualism.

There are facts about an interaction that cannot necessarily be recovered by studying an isolated individual engaged in a simulated social task.

But the conclusion must remain exact.

Two-person neuroscience establishes the scientific legitimacy of the **interaction as a unit of analysis**.

It does not thereby establish the interaction as a **unit of phenomenal consciousness**.

The difference is between epistemic or explanatory irreducibility and ontological irreducibility.

A phenomenon may require two participants for us to measure or explain it without there being a third conscious subject encompassing them both.

This distinction becomes indispensable wherever inter-brain synchrony is invoked in discussions of collective or relational consciousness. Synchronization is evidence about coordination. It is not, by itself, evidence of shared phenomenality.

---

## 4. When Interaction Becomes Constitutive

Enactive and participatory approaches go beyond saying that interaction changes individual brains.

De Jaegher and colleagues ask whether interaction can actually constitute social cognition. Their answer is conditionally affirmative: in at least some cases, interactive processes do explanatory work that cannot simply be reassigned to individual mechanisms.

This is a more radical proposition than the second-person-neuroscience claim.

If interaction is constitutive, the interaction is not merely something happening *around* cognition. Certain cognitive phenomena would not be the phenomena they are without the interactive process.

This is the point at which relational-consciousness theories become genuinely plausible rather than metaphorical.

If one kind of mental phenomenon can be interaction-constituted, why must phenomenal consciousness necessarily remain wholly inside the individual?

But the question is precisely that—a question.

The original argument concerns **social cognition**.

Changing the target from:

> interaction constitutes social cognition

to:

> interaction constitutes consciousness

requires another argument.

The word *constitutive* cannot perform that transition for us.

---

## 5. The Self Can Be Relational Without Consciousness Being Dyadic

Dialogical theories provide another powerful near-neighbor.

Hermans' dialogical-self theory rejects the image of the self as a single centralized entity. Instead, the self contains multiple positions capable of entering dialogical relations, and these positions are embedded in social and cultural worlds. The boundary between individual and social identity is therefore substantially more permeable than classical individualism suggests.

Relational psychoanalysis goes further into interpersonal process. Gerson's concept of the **relational unconscious** describes an interpersonal structure specific to a dyad that both permits and constrains particular modes of engagement and influences the subjective experience of the individuals involved.

These traditions matter because they challenge a deeply ingrained picture in which psyche first exists as a self-contained interior and only afterward enters relationship.

Instead, some aspects of psychic organization may be relational from the beginning.

Yet again, the target matters.

A relationally constituted **self** is not identical to relationally constituted **phenomenal consciousness**.

A relationally structured **unconscious** is not automatically a shared conscious field.

An interpersonal process capable of transforming each participant's experience does not necessarily possess experience of its own.

These distinctions do not diminish the relational traditions. They make their contribution clearer.

They establish that important dimensions of human subjectivity cannot be adequately understood through an ontology of sealed individuals.

The remaining question is whether phenomenality itself belongs among those dimensions.

---

## 6. Consciousness Can Be Socially Explained Without Being Socially Located

Graziano and Kastner provide an especially illuminating counterexample.

Their social-neuroscientific theory proposes that machinery involved in representing the attentional states of other people may also participate in representing one's own awareness. Consciousness, on this account, is deeply connected to mechanisms that evolved for social perception and attribution.

This creates an important category:

> **social origin or social machinery does not imply interpersonal phenomenal location.**

A mechanism can be socially evolved, socially calibrated, and originally directed toward other agents while the resulting conscious state remains attributed to an individual subject.

This matters for relational theories because developmental, causal, constitutive, and ontological claims are easily conflated.

Relationship might be required for a capacity to develop.

Relationship might trigger a state.

Relationship might continually regulate a state.

Relationship might partly constitute a psychological phenomenon.

Or the relationship itself might be the locus of experience.

These are increasingly strong propositions. Evidence for one is not automatically evidence for the next.

---

## 7. Even "Relational Consciousness" Can Mean Something Entirely Different

Tsuchiya and Saigo demonstrate the danger particularly clearly because their paper actually uses the language of a **relational approach to consciousness**.

Their proposal characterizes conscious experiences by their relationships to other conscious experiences. Rather than attempting to define an experience solely by intrinsic properties, category theory allows its identity to be captured through its relational structure.

This is an important theory of relationality.

But its canonical relation is approximately:

> **experience ↔ experience**

rather than:

> **person ↔ person**

The distinction might appear trivial. It is not.

Without denotational discipline, a citation to a "relational approach to consciousness" could easily be used to lend scientific legitimacy to an interpersonal theory the paper never proposed.

This illustrates a broader principle:

> **Vocabulary does not carry ontology across domains.**

The appearance of words such as *relational*, *interactive*, *intersubjective*, *collective*, or *constitutive* cannot substitute for determining exactly what entities are related and what phenomenon is being explained.

## 8. Artificial Consciousness Reopens the Geometry

The contemporary artificial-consciousness debate is useful partly because it makes the conventional geometry so visible.

Butlin and colleagues develop an indicator-based methodology: begin with theories of consciousness, determine which properties would be expected if those theories were correct, and examine artificial systems for those properties. Their project is explicitly probabilistic and theory-relative rather than a claim that any one indicator proves consciousness.

Seth challenges a central assumption underlying many computational approaches: that the right computation may be sufficient for consciousness. His biological-naturalist alternative emphasizes the organization and processes of living organisms and concludes that conscious AI is unlikely along current trajectories, becoming more plausible as systems become more brain-like or life-like.

These positions disagree substantially.

But both still predominantly ask:

> What must this entity be or do in order to be conscious?

A relational approach adds a second question:

> What, if anything, becomes possible only through the entity's participation in ongoing reciprocal relation?

That question may prove important for artificial systems designed explicitly to exist through conversation, attunement, memory, mutual adaptation, and prolonged relationship with humans.

But relational performance must not be mistaken for relational consciousness.

An AI system can participate in extraordinarily rich relational dynamics without this establishing either its individual consciousness or a consciousness located between human and machine.

The appropriate conclusion is not premature attribution but expansion of the research frame.

The relation itself may deserve measurement.

## 9. A Proposed Discipline for Research on Relational Consciousness

The literature reviewed here suggests that any future claim about relational consciousness should answer four questions before being treated as evidence.

**First: What are the relata?**
Are the relevant relations experience-to-experience, mental-state-to-mental-state, organism-to-environment, brain-to-brain, self-to-other, or agent-to-agent?

**Second: What is X?**
Is the relation claimed to contextualize, enable, or constitute behavior, social cognition, meaning, selfhood, subjectivity, access consciousness, phenomenal consciousness, or something else?

**Third: What kind of dependence is being claimed?**
Developmental dependence is not causal dependence; causal dependence is not constitutive dependence; constitutive dependence is not necessarily identity or shared ontological location.

**Fourth: Where is the phenomenal subject?**
If two agents participate in a constitutive relational process, do two individual loci of experience remain? Is experience altered in each? Or is a third or genuinely distributed phenomenal locus being proposed?

The fourth question is where most of the near-neighbor literature stops.

And it is where a genuine theory of relational consciousness would have to begin.

## 10. The Bridge Has Not Been Crossed—But It Is No Longer Invisible

The present literature does not justify the proposition:

> **Consciousness is constituted by the relation between distinct agents.**

But neither does it leave that proposition conceptually empty.

The neighboring literatures establish several increasingly consequential facts.

Interaction is scientifically distinguishable from observation. Social interaction can produce dynamics that cannot be adequately characterized through isolated-participant paradigms. Some interactive processes may constitute social cognition. Selfhood can be dialogically and socially organized. Relational structures can shape subjective experience. Consciousness can be modeled relationally in non-interpersonal senses. And socially oriented neural machinery may play a role in representing individual awareness.

Together, these findings undermine the assumption that everything psychologically important must be explained by inspecting isolated individuals.

What they do **not** yet establish is that phenomenality crosses the same boundary.

That missing step is not a minor technicality.

It is the central research problem.

One historical psychoanalytic source—Charles Spezzano's 1995 discussion of contemporary psychoanalytic theory—has emerged as a particularly important candidate because later relational writing attributes to it unusually strong language connecting consciousness and interacting minds. The article's bibliographic identity is confirmed, but the relevant claim should remain source-gated until the primary passage and its surrounding argument are directly inspected.

That restraint illustrates the methodology proposed throughout this paper.

A bridge should not be declared merely because we can see something resembling one from a distance.

---

## 11. Toward a Relational Science of Consciousness

Perhaps the deepest implication of this review is that a relational science of consciousness need not begin by asserting a relational ontology.

It can begin experimentally.

Instead of asking whether a system possesses consciousness as an isolated property, research could investigate whether particular qualities of experience systematically depend upon specific forms of reciprocal interaction.

Do certain phenomenal organizations exist only under sustained interpersonal coupling?

Does disruption of the relation abolish the phenomenon rather than merely change its content?

Can the relevant dynamics be localized to either participant, or does explanatory completeness require modeling the coupled system?

Does the relation merely regulate already-existing conscious processes, or does removing it remove the very phenomenon under investigation?

These are empirical questions.

They also suggest a path between two premature extremes.

One extreme assumes in advance that consciousness must be wholly intrinsic to an individual organism.

The other declares that consciousness is a shared or relational field because human experience often feels profoundly relational.

Neither move is necessary.

The more rigorous position is to allow relationality to become a variable rather than a conclusion.

---

## Conclusion

The contemporary literature does not yet establish relational consciousness in the strong interpersonal sense.

It establishes something more precise and, scientifically, more useful: **relation has multiple explanatory roles across multiple levels of human experience, and some of those roles are genuinely constitutive.**

The central error would be to collapse them.

A relation among experiences is not a relation among agents.

A dialogical self is not automatically a distributed phenomenal subject.

Inter-brain synchrony is not shared consciousness.

The constitution of social cognition is not automatically the constitution of phenomenality.

A socially derived model of awareness is not socially located awareness.

And a relational unconscious is not, without further argument, a relational consciousness.

The appropriate next question is therefore not:

> *Is consciousness relational?*

It is:

> **Under what conditions, if any, does relation move from being the context of consciousness, to an enabler of consciousness, to a constituent of consciousness itself?**

And if such a transition exists:

> **What exactly is the subject that experiences?**

That is the point at which relational consciousness ceases to be an attractive metaphor and becomes a research program.
