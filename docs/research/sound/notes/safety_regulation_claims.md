# Safety, Regulation and Claim Discipline for Consumer Sound/Entrainment Wellness Products

*Research notes, compiled 2026-11-09. This is research, not legal advice. Items marked **[COUNSEL]** need a qualified regulatory or IP lawyer. Items marked **[BACKGROUND, NOT VERIFIED THIS SESSION]** come from the researcher's prior knowledge and were not re-fetched; the report writer should treat them as lower-confidence. Most sources below are law-firm or trade summaries rather than the primary regulatory text. Where that matters, it is flagged.*

**Binding vs guidance key:** BINDING = statute, regulation or court order. GUIDANCE = an agency's non-binding statement of policy or enforcement intent. INDUSTRY = standards, contracts or platform terms (binding only between the parties).

## 1. Safety evidence: seizures, hearing, sleep with headphones, vulnerable groups, device interference

### Takeaway
Seizure risk is well established for **visual** flicker (15–25 Hz is the most provocative range). For **audio-only** entrainment, including binaural beats, there is no published evidence of seizures. The risk is theoretical, and rare musicogenic epilepsy is the edge case. The best-evidenced hazard for audio products is noise dose (volume × time) under the WHO-ITU H.870 safe-listening standard. Sleeping in earbuds carries mostly minor, poorly studied risks: irritation, infection, wax impaction, and missing alarms. Warnings about psychosis, dissociation, PTSD, pregnancy and pacemakers rest almost entirely on vendor and blog sources, not clinical evidence.

### Cited Findings
**Visual/audiovisual seizure risk (well established)**
- About 0.3–3% of the population shows a photoparoxysmal EEG response to light or pattern. Light-provoked seizures occur in about 1 in 10,000 people overall, or about 1 in 4,000 aged 5–24 (Fisher et al., *Epilepsia* 2005, Epilepsy Foundation of America working-group review). — [Fisher et al. 2005 review](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/photic-and-pattern-induced-seizures-a-review-for-the-epilepsy-fou/); [updated EFA review](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/visually-sensitive-seizures-an-updated-review-by-the-epilepsy-fou/)
- Among people with epilepsy, 2–14% may have seizures triggered by light or pattern. Flash rates of 15–25 Hz are the most provocative, but the provocative range spans 1–65 Hz. Red flashes and oscillating stripes add risk. — same sources ([Aston](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/photic-and-pattern-induced-seizures-a-review-for-the-epilepsy-fou/))
- Risk-reduction guidelines for flashing content have been issued by UK, Japanese and ITU bodies. — [EFA review summary](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/visually-sensitive-seizures-an-updated-review-by-the-epilepsy-fou/)
- *Implication:* any light/sound machine, flicker goggles, or 40 Hz audiovisual product (see Cognito below) falls within this established risk class.

**Auditory-only seizure risk (theoretical)**
- Musicogenic epilepsy is a rare reflex epilepsy triggered by music. The estimated prevalence is about 1 in 10 million, and it may be under-reported. — [Epilepsy Society UK](https://epilepsysociety.org.uk/node/3175); [PubMed review 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25725912/)
- Brain.fm, a vendor, states it knows of no published evidence linking binaural beats to seizures, says a theoretical risk cannot be excluded, and names volume and duration through headphones as the main practical hazards. This is a vendor source, not independent. — [Brain.fm blog](https://www.brain.fm/blog/binaural-beats-safety)
- No peer-reviewed study testing binaural/isochronic beats as a seizure trigger was found.

**Hearing safety (standard-backed)**
- WHO-ITU **H.870** "Safe listening devices and systems" (2018; ITU ref T-REC-H.870-201808). Adult mode allows **80 dBA for 40 h/week**; a children/sensitive mode uses **75 dBA**. Exposure follows energy equivalence, so +3 dB halves the allowed time (for example, 89 dBA allows about 5 h/week). The standard calls for dose tracking as a percentage of a weekly "sound allowance," user prompts, and automatic volume reduction. — [WHO publication page](https://www.who.int/publications-detail/safe-listening-devices-and-systems-a-who-itu-standard); [Hearing Review summary](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-loss/hearing-loss-prevention/risk-factors/standards-for-safe-listening-past-present-and-future)
- One secondary source states 80 h/week, which conflicts with the other sources and appears to be a typo. — [Canadian Audiologist](https://canadianaudiologist.ca/safe-listening/?output=pdf)
- H.870 is a **voluntary standard (INDUSTRY)**, not law.

**Sleeping with headphones/earbuds**
- Cleveland Clinic lists pain and pressure for side sleepers, skin damage in the ear canal from poorly fitting buds, moisture and infection, wax impaction, and missing alarms or emergencies (worse with noise cancelling). It recommends over-ear or headband-style sleep headphones and low volume. — [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/sleeping-with-headphones)
- A small study cited by Johns Hopkins ENT Carrie Nieman linked frequent earbud use to skin irritation, which was associated with infection. She described the study as limited. — [The Star, 2026](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/05/19/is-it-safe-to-wear-earbuds-while-sleeping)
- Reports of "AirPods ear" pressure wounds in the canal are uncited anecdotes. — [Medical Dialogues](https://mhealth.medicaldialogues.in/article/health-topics/airpods-ear-ent-surgeons-report-rising-ear-canal-injuries-from-prolonged-earbud-use-dr-deepti-koganti-162387)

**Vulnerable groups and device interference (weak evidence)**
- Dr Weil's site says adverse events from binaural beats appear "extremely rare." It cites a theoretical concern for epilepsy and for irregular heartbeat or pacemakers, and notes that warnings are partly a liability measure. — [DrWeil.com](https://www.drweil.com/health-wellness/balanced-living/technology/beware-of-binaural-therapy)
- On pregnancy, sources conflict. A safety guide says there is insufficient research. A vendor says there is "no evidence" of danger. Neither is independent. — [Mystic Ryst guide](https://mysticryst.com/blogs/the-mystic-journal/brainwave-entrainment-is-it-safe-complete-safety-guide); [binauralbeatsmeditation.com](https://www.binauralbeatsmeditation.com/is-it-safe-to-use-binaural-beats-during-pregnancy/)
- Typical existing-product warnings, assembled from vendor pages: do not use while driving or operating machinery; consult a physician if you have epilepsy, a seizure history, a heart condition or pacemaker, are pregnant, or have psychiatric conditions; keep volume low. — [binauralbeatsmeditation.com epilepsy page](https://www.binauralbeatsmeditation.com/binaural-beats-epilepsy-seizures/); [DrWeil.com](https://www.drweil.com/health-wellness/balanced-living/technology/beware-of-binaural-therapy)

### Inferences
- For an audio-only product, a defensible safety posture rests on: (a) H.870-style volume and dose management; (b) a "not while driving" warning, since relaxation and sleep induction impair alertness; (c) a precautionary epilepsy, pacemaker and pregnancy notice framed honestly as precaution rather than demonstrated hazard. Adding **any visual flicker** moves the product into an evidenced seizure-risk class and should trigger ITU/Ofcom-style flash limits.
- Psychosis, dissociation and PTSD: a deeply relaxing or trance-inducing session has a plausible but unstudied risk for dissociation-prone users (this is reasoning by analogy with meditation adverse-event literature, not something found here). A precautionary "stop if distressing; this is not treatment" line is prudent.
- For vibration/vibroacoustic products, consult the specific device's labeling. The pacemaker concern is theoretical, and no sourced data was found.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study testing auditory beats as a seizure trigger was found. PubMed was not searched directly.
- No clinical guidance was found on binaural or entrainment audio for psychosis, dissociation or PTSD (the meditation adverse-event literature, e.g. Britton/Lindahl, was not searched).
- No vibroacoustic or pacemaker interference data or labeling was found.
- No peer-reviewed case reports of ear-canal pressure necrosis from overnight earbuds were found.
- The ITU flash guideline (BT.1702) and Ofcom/Harding rule numbers were not fetched.

## 2. US regulation: FDA general wellness, SaMD line, cleared precedents, FTC enforcement

### Takeaway
FDA's **General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices** (GUIDANCE, revised **January 6, 2026**, replacing the 2019 version) still applies a two-part test. The product must (1) be intended only for general wellness and (2) be low risk. A sound app claiming to relax, aid sleep quality, focus or manage stress generally sits under enforcement discretion. Claims to treat, diagnose or mitigate a disease or condition (insomnia disorder, ADHD, tinnitus, Alzheimer's, PTSD, anxiety disorder) turn it into a device. Cleared sound and neuro-digital precedents went through De Novo with RCT evidence: EndeavorRx (2020), Lenire (2023), and Cognito Spectris (pivotal trial, not yet cleared as of sources found). The FTC enforces truth in advertising separately (BINDING FTC Act §5), and its 2022 guidance expects RCT-grade substantiation for health benefit claims.

### Cited Findings
**FDA general wellness (GUIDANCE)**
- FDA issued the updated guidance on **January 6, 2026**, replacing 2019. The two-factor framework (general wellness intent plus low risk) is unchanged. The main expansion is that non-invasive sensors estimating physiologic parameters can now qualify. — [Covington, Jan 2026](https://cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products); [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/fda-adapts-with-the-times-on-digital-health-updated-guidances-on-general-wellness-products); [Faegre Drinker](https://faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/1/key-updates-in-fdas-2026-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)
- At a town hall, FDA said a product may make wellness claims but still fall outside the policy if its technology is not low risk. Invasive or implanted products and lasers or radiation are excluded. A microneedle glucose wearable was given as an example that is "not low risk" despite wellness claims. — [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/fda-town-hall-sheds-new-light-on-updated-general-wellness-policy); [Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/01/fda-updates-its-general-wellness-device-frameworkwhat-companies-need-to-know)
- FDA revised the Clinical Decision Support guidance on the same date and again on **January 29, 2026**. — [King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/insights/articles/fda-updates-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-guidance-documents)
- Commentators say the boundary between standard devices and general wellness devices is still unclear. — [Honigman](https://www.honigman.com/alert-3236)
- [BACKGROUND, NOT VERIFIED THIS SESSION] The guidance's two categories of acceptable claims are (a) maintaining or encouraging a general state of health (e.g., relaxation, stress management, sleep management, mental acuity) without reference to disease, and (b) claims that healthy lifestyle choices may reduce the risk or impact of certain chronic diseases where that association is well understood. The primary FDA text should be fetched to quote exact language.

**Cleared/authorized precedents**
- **EndeavorRx** (Akili) received a De Novo grant on **June 15, 2020**. It was the first game-based digital therapeutic, Rx-only, for ages 8–12 with ADHD. The evidence base was 5 studies with more than 600 children. In the RCT, 47% vs 32% improved on TOVA. Adverse events included frustration, headache, dizziness, emotional reaction and aggression. The De Novo grant created a new classification that later products can use via 510(k). — [Akili/PR Newswire 2020](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/fda-permits-marketing-of-first-game-based-digital-therapeutic-to-improve-attention-function-in-children-with-adhd-301077106.html); [MedTech Dive](https://www.medtechdive.com/news/in-fda-1st-game-based-therapeutic-gets-marketing-ok/579839/)
- **Lenire** (Neuromod) received a De Novo grant in **March 2023** for tinnitus. It combines bimodal sound through headphones with electrical tongue stimulation, is prescribed via audiologists or ENTs, and is used 60 min/day. The TENT-A3 trial (n=112) compared it with sound therapy alone, and the company reports 79.4% clinically significant improvement. These are manufacturer figures. — [Hearing Review](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-loss/tinnitus/neuromod-devices-lenire-receives-de-novo-approval-from-fda); [Lenire](https://www.lenire.com/lenire-granted-fda-approval/)
- **Cognito Therapeutics Spectris (40 Hz gamma audiovisual stimulation, Alzheimer's)**: the HOPE pivotal trial (NCT05637801) completed enrollment of 670 in **July 2025**. It is a 12-month, sham-controlled, double-blind home-use study. The earlier OVERTURE trial (n=76, 6 months) treated efficacy outcomes as exploratory. The company's claims of 76–77% reductions in decline come from post hoc analyses. No HOPE topline result or FDA authorization was found through November 2026. — [BusinessWire Jul 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250701375054/en/Cognito-Therapeutics-Completes-Enrollment-in-HOPE-Pivotal-Study-of-Spectris-AD-Therapy-for-the-Treatment-of-Patients-with-Alzheimers-Disease); [Frontiers in Neurology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2024.1343588); [Clinical Trials Arena](https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/cognitos-sensory-stimulation-device-slows-decline-in-alzheimers/)

**FTC (BINDING statute; GUIDANCE on substantiation)**
- **Lumos Labs (Lumosity), January 2016**: paid $2M in consumer redress, with a $50M judgment suspended. The FTC alleged deceptive claims that brain training improved everyday performance, delayed age-related cognitive decline and helped with conditions including ADHD and PTSD. It also alleged undisclosed incentivized testimonials. The founders must have competent scientific evidence for future claims. The FTC called it the first government settlement with a brain-health app maker. — [FTC press release](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges); [Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/?p=239313)
- **FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (December 2022, GUIDANCE)** replaced the 1998 supplements guide and expressly covers devices and apps. Health claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence," with RCTs on the product (or an essentially equivalent one) as the general expectation. Animal studies, in vitro studies, anecdotes and testimonials are insufficient. Commentators disagree on whether two RCTs are required. The guidance is non-binding, and critics say it exceeds case law. — [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide); [King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/its-not-just-for-dietary-supplements-anymore-ftc-revises-and-expands-guidance-for-health-claims); [WLF critique](https://www.wlf.org/?p=19499)

### Inferences
- The FDA line runs on **intended use**, shown by labeling, ads, app-store copy and even founder statements. A wellness audio app becomes SaMD by naming a disease or disorder, or by claiming to treat or diagnose. Low risk is necessary but not sufficient.
- FDA enforcement discretion does **not** protect against FTC liability. Even a wellness claim such as "improves sleep" or "boosts focus" is an efficacy claim needing substantiation proportionate to the claim. Lumosity shows that cognitive and age-decline claims draw enforcement.
- Every cleared sound or neuro precedent relied on RCTs and Rx/clinician channels. That is the benchmark if a product ever wants disease claims. **[COUNSEL]** for any claim referencing insomnia, anxiety, tinnitus, ADHD, dementia or PTSD.

### Gaps
- No FTC action was found that specifically targets a sound, binaural or "frequency healing" product. The search was not targeted enough to establish whether one exists.
- Somryst (insomnia PDT) and any cleared sleep audio device were not confirmed this session.
- The primary FDA 2026 guidance text was not fetched, so the claim examples above come from summaries.
- No FDA warning letters to sound or frequency apps were searched.

## 3. EU and UK: MDR borderline, MHRA, consumer protection, EU AI Act

### Takeaway
In the EU, software is a medical device if its manufacturer-stated intended purpose is medical. **MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 (June 2025, GUIDANCE)** governs qualification, and Rule 11 (Annex VIII of the BINDING MDR 2017/745) classifies medical software at class IIa or higher. The UK MHRA (UK MDR 2002 as amended, BINDING) likewise keys on intended purpose. Its **February 2025** digital mental health guidance stresses that functionality counts as well as stated intent. Separately, the **EU AI Act Art. 50** transparency obligations (BINDING, applicable **2 August 2026**) require providers of generative AI to mark AI-generated audio in machine-readable form, and require deployers to label deepfake audio. A voluntary Code of Practice was finalized on **10 June 2026**.

### Cited Findings
- **MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1** was published on **17 June 2025**. Commentators say the qualification logic and Rule 11 structure are unchanged, but it adds examples. Summaries disagree on wellness and lifestyle apps: one says purely wellness software is excluded, another stresses that some non-medical software can be caught. One source says Rule 11's reading of "prevention" was widened, pushing predictive tools to IIb. — [Emergo by UL](https://www.emergobyul.com/news/european-revision-primary-software-guidance-mdcg-2019-11-revision-1-small-changes-meaningful); [MDR Regulator](https://mdrregulator.com/news/revised-mdcg-2019-11-guidance-updated-approach-to-qualification-and-classification-of-medical-software); [OpenRegulatory](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2019-11)
- **MDCG 2025-4** (June 2025) covers online platforms and app stores. It recommends a prominent "Medical Device" category to separate certified apps from wellness apps, and confirms that app stores and developers are subject to medical device law. — [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/eu-confirms-app-stores-and-developers-are-subject-medical-device-laws)
- **MHRA**: guidance on digital mental health technology qualification and classification was published on **3 February 2025**. The key distinction is "support wellbeing" vs "aid treatment of a mental health condition." Commentators warn that a wellbeing app adding AI mood analysis or risk stratification may become a higher-class SaMD. The Software and AI as a Medical Device Change Programme (since October 2022) addresses the "SaMD vs wellbeing and lifestyle" boundary, and the MHRA offers a software flowchart. Post-market surveillance rules came into force in June 2025. — [GOV.UK DMHT guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-mental-health-technology-qualification-and-classification); [GOV.UK roadmap](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/software-and-ai-as-a-medical-device-change-programme/software-and-ai-as-a-medical-device-change-programme-roadmap); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/synapse/2025/ai-in-life-sciences/uk-approach-to-regulating-ai-in-life-sciences)
- **EU AI Act Art. 50**: obligations apply from **2 August 2026**. Under Art. 50(2), providers must mark synthetic audio as machine-readable and detectable, for example with an imperceptible watermark. Deployers must label deepfakes, which Art. 3(60) defines as content resembling existing persons, objects, places or events. For audio-only content, the Code suggests audio disclaimers. The Code was finalized on **10 June 2026**, and the Commission found it adequate on **8 July 2026**. Signing is voluntary, but non-signatories remain bound by Art. 50. Icons cover "fully AI-generated" and "partially AI-modified." Penalties reportedly reach up to 3% of turnover. — [Jones Day, Jun 2026](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/european-commission-publishes-final-code-of-practice-on-marking-and-labelling-aigenerated-content); [Lewis Silkin, Jul 2026](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/07/24/the-eus-new-ai-labelling-rules-what-every-organisation-needs-to-know-102ne35); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/eu-ai-content-rules-force-publishers-to-label-or-risk-3-of-turnover/)
- [BACKGROUND, NOT VERIFIED THIS SESSION] EU consumer protection: the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive 2005/29/EC (BINDING) Annex I item 17 blacklists falsely claiming a product can cure illnesses. In the UK, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (consumer provisions in force April 2025) replaced the CPRs 2008 and carries a similar blacklist, with the CMA able to fine directly. The CAP Code (ASA, self-regulatory) section 12 restricts medical claims and requires documentary evidence, and the ASA has a long record of rulings against unsubstantiated sleep and relaxation app claims.

### Inferences
- A generative or adaptive sound engine is unlikely to be a *high-risk* AI system under the AI Act unless it is part of a medical device (Annex I via MDR). The main AI Act duties are Art. 50 output marking (provider) and deepfake labeling. A deepfake label is relevant only if the product generates audio resembling real persons, such as voice clones of named teachers. **[COUNSEL]** on provider vs deployer role, especially if a third-party generator is used.
- EU/UK and US converge: intended purpose plus actual functionality decide device status. Adding mood scoring, risk stratification or "anxiety reduction" features can tip a wellness app into SaMD in the UK and EU.

### Gaps
- The primary MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 text was not read, so the treatment of wellness examples remains disputed across summaries.
- ASA/CAP rulings on sound, sleep or binaural apps were not retrieved.
- The DMCC Act and UCPD details were not verified this session.
- No search was made for national (Germany, France) enforcement against wellness audio.

## 4. Copyright and licensing: AI music, training data, recorded performances, third-party embeds

### Takeaway
The US Copyright Office says AI training is **not categorically fair use**. Market harm (factor 4) is often decisive, and unlawfully sourced data or market-competing outputs weigh against fair use (Part 3, pre-publication, **May 9, 2025**: GUIDANCE/analysis, not law). The label litigation is resolving through **licensing settlements**: UMG–Udio (October 2025), WMG–Udio (November 2025) and WMG–Suno (November 2025). UMG and Sony v. Suno remained active as of mid-2026, and Sony filed a new suit against Udio in July 2026. Platform terms (BINDING contracts) prohibit using Spotify content for ML training and prohibit isolating or modifying YouTube audio.

### Cited Findings
- **USCO Part 3 (Generative AI Training), pre-publication May 9, 2025**: fair use depends on context and degree. Training on large, diverse datasets is often transformative, but factor 4 (market harm) is frequently decisive. Pirated sources and outputs that compete with the originals cut against fair use. No new exception was proposed. Register Shira Perlmutter was dismissed shortly after release. — [Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-Copyright-Office-Issues-Key-Guidance-on-Fair-Use-in-Generative-AI-Training); [Sidley](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2025/05/28/generative-ai-meets-copyright-scrutiny-highlights-from-the-copyright-offices-part-iii-report/); [Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/us-copyright-office-ai-report-part-3-what-authors-should-know/)
- [BACKGROUND, NOT VERIFIED THIS SESSION] USCO Part 2 (Copyrightability, January 2025) says purely AI-generated output is not copyrightable, while human selection, arrangement or modification can be protected. *Thaler v. Perlmutter* (D.C. Cir., March 2025) affirmed the human authorship requirement. See [copyright.gov/ai](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/).
- **UMG–Udio** settled in **October 2025** with an undisclosed compensatory payment and a licensing deal for a new AI creation platform. — [CMU](https://completemusicupdate.com/universal-has-settled-its-lawsuit-with-udio-and-signed-ai-deals-with-udio-and-stability/)
- **WMG–Udio** settled on **19 November 2025**. A licensed platform is planned for 2026, and content cannot be downloaded or shared off-platform. — [AJC/AP](https://www.ajc.com/news/2025/11/warner-music-and-ai-startup-udio-settle-copyright-battle-and-ink-license-deal)
- **WMG–Suno** settled in late **November 2025**. Suno will train new models only on licensed WMG catalog, artists opt in to the use of their name, likeness and voice, Suno acquires Songkick, and terms were undisclosed. — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/warner-music-settles-lawsuit-with-ai-music-startup-suno); [MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/music-tech/artists-and-songwriters-will-have-full-control-over-whether-and-how-their-names-images-likenesses-voices-and-compositions-are-used-in-new-ai-generated-music-warners-and-suno-link-up-to-create-new-legal-ai-platforms)
- **UMG and Sony v. Suno** (D. Mass.) remained active. An April 2026 report describes a settlement impasse over fees and equity. Sony also continues against Udio and reportedly filed a new suit on **20 July 2026** over more than 30,000 recordings. These are secondary sources (AI Law Wiki, Dynamoi) and are unconfirmed in primary dockets. — [AI Law Wiki](https://ailawwiki.com/News_UMG_Sony_Suno_Settlement_Impasse_April_2026); [Dynamoi timeline](https://dynamoi.com/learn/ai-music-distribution/ai-music-copyright-cases-timeline)
- **Spotify** Developer Policy (effective 15 May 2025): "Do not use the Spotify Platform or any Spotify Content to train a machine learning or AI model." Spotify staff on its forum say apps may not synchronize music to visual media. — [Spotify Developer Policy](https://developer.spotify.com/policy/); [Spotify community](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Spotify-for-Developers/What-legally-counts-as-synchronization-of-music/m-p/5070851)
- **YouTube API** policies prohibit separating or isolating audio or video components, offering download or audio separation, letting users modify the audio or video, and overlays or modifications to the embedded player. — [YouTube developer policies guide](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies-guide); [Required Minimum Functionality](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/required-minimum-functionality)

### Inferences
- A product that layers entrainment tones over, or processes, YouTube or Spotify audio would likely breach platform terms. Embedding an unmodified player is the permitted pattern. Entrainment audio should come from owned, commissioned or licensed recordings, which need both master and composition rights. **[COUNSEL]**
- Generated music may be uncopyrightable without human authorship, which limits a product's ability to protect its outputs. Using a third-party generator raises training-data provenance risk; the label-licensed models emerging from the 2025 settlements are the lower-risk route.

### Gaps
- The USCO Part 3 final (non-pre-publication) status was not confirmed.
- No reporting after mid-2026 on UMG/Sony v. Suno was found.
- SoundCloud API terms were not retrieved.
- Court rulings on training fair use (Bartz v. Anthropic, Kadrey v. Meta, 2025) were not searched for this note.

## 5. Best-practice claim discipline

### Takeaway
Reputable wellness audio stays in FDA's "general state of health" lane, using verbs such as *support, help you relax, designed to, may help*. It avoids naming diseases or disorders and avoids claims of guaranteed or quantified outcomes unless an RCT on the product backs them. Under FTC guidance, even soft efficacy claims ("improves focus by X%") need product-specific evidence, and testimonials cannot carry claims the evidence cannot.

### Cited Findings
- FDA's wellness framework accepts claims about relaxation, stress, sleep and mental acuity without disease reference. Disease, treatment and diagnosis language makes the product a device. — [Covington 2026](https://cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)
- The Lumosity order targeted claims about everyday performance, age-related decline, ADHD and PTSD, plus undisclosed incentivized testimonials. — [FTC 2016](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges)
- The FTC 2022 guidance says anecdotes and testimonials are insufficient and RCTs on the product are the norm. — [Covington 2023](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide)
- MHRA's line is "support wellbeing" (not a device) vs "aid treatment of a mental health condition" (device). — [GOV.UK DMHT](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-mental-health-technology-qualification-and-classification)

### Inferences (claim ladder, synthesized from the above)
- **Generally safer (wellness, still needs reasonable basis):** "designed to support relaxation"; "a soundscape for winding down"; "may help you settle before sleep"; "sounds to accompany focus sessions."
- **Requires product-specific RCT substantiation (FTC), even if FDA-exempt:** "improves sleep quality," "reduces stress," "boosts focus by 30%," "clinically proven," "scientifically designed to change your brainwaves," and any quantified outcome.
- **Device/medical claims, prohibited without clearance (FDA/MDR/MHRA):** treats or helps insomnia, anxiety, depression, PTSD, ADHD, tinnitus, migraine or dementia; "alternative to medication"; diagnostic or "detects your stress disorder" features.
- **Also risky:** "entrains your brain to 40 Hz gamma," which borrows from Cognito's Alzheimer's research and implies disease benefit; references to Alzheimer's or cognitive decline studies in marketing; healing-frequency (432/528 Hz) cure claims; undisclosed paid testimonials.
