## Core Product Principle

The user enters through different intentions:

* Write
* Book
* Idea
* Journal
* Research
* Create something to share

These are not separate products.
They are entry modes into one consistent environment:

**Writer Canvas**

The Writer Canvas is the product identity.
The creator should learn:

> "Whatever I am creating, this is where my work develops."

The Writer Canvas is the environment where creative work develops.
The writing surface is one instrument inside that environment.

**Do not begin by designing the editor. Begin by designing the creative field.**

## Important Distinction

Do not design:

* a book editor;
* a blog editor;
* a journal app;

**one creative environment with different modes.**

## Experience Design Principles

The Writer Canvas must feel like a professional creative studio, not a document form.

The user should immediately understand:

* What am I working on?
* What materials are feeding this?
* What stage of creation am I in?
* Where do I continue?

The system provides **orientation, not measurement**.

Creative stages may include:

```
Capture → Gather → Develop → Shape → Create → Refine → Share
```

These are not progress metrics. They are ways of understanding where the work is —
how work moves through the room, not how the person is scored.

**Begin with the room.**
Define the spatial experience before defining components.

Example spatial zones:

```
Context   | Creative Field | Intelligence

Materials | Work           | MAIA
```

## Mode Fluidity

Do not let the modes become rigid categories.

A person may enter as:

> "I have an idea."

Three months later it becomes:

> "Now this is a book."

Six months later:

> "Now this is a course."

The Writer Canvas has to preserve that evolution.
**The mode is the starting posture, not the identity of the work.**

## Expanded Writer Canvas Capabilities (founder amendment, 2026-08-05)

**The Writer Canvas supports creative continuity, not just creation.**
Real writers enter at many points in the lifecycle. A writer may arrive with:

* a blank idea;
* a half-finished manuscript from 10 years ago;
* a dissertation proposal;
* a thesis chapter;
* a collection of journals;
* a collaborative writing project;
* a book they abandoned;
* a published work they want to transform into something new.

These are different **entry conditions** into the same environment.

A creator may enter at any stage:

```text
Capture → Develop → Draft → Revise → Renew → Collaborate → Complete → Share
```

The Studio does not assume every work begins as a new blank page.

### Additional Creative Modes — where someone is, not only what they make

**1. New Creation** — "I have something emerging."
For new ideas, essays, blogs, stories, books, courses.

**2. Renew an Existing Work** — "I have something that wants another life."
For old manuscripts, abandoned books, previous writings, unpublished material, archived ideas.
Capabilities: import older manuscript · preserve original version · create a renewed working
version · compare old and new · rediscover themes · continue development.
**Important principle: the original work is preserved. Renewal is not overwriting.**

Renewal is not `open old file → edit → save`. It is:
*encounter an earlier self's work → preserve what existed → discover what wants to continue →
create a new expression.*

The model:

```text
Original Work
      |
New Expression
      |
Renewed Work
```

not:

```text
Old manuscript
      |
modified manuscript
```

**3. Collaborative Writing** — "I am creating with others."
For writing partners, co-authors, editors, research collaborators, dissertation advisors.
Capabilities: invite collaborator · define role · shared workspace · comments/reflections ·
version history · attribution.
**Collaboration must preserve authorship.** The system should know who contributed, who edited,
who approved changes.

**4. Academic Writing** — "I am developing rigorous research."
For PhD dissertations, master's theses, research papers, scholarly projects. Its own pathway
because the process differs: research organization, references, citations, literature review,
chapter structure, advisor feedback, revision cycles, defense preparation.
**But it still lives inside Writer Canvas. Not a separate dissertation app.**

### Updated Writer Canvas Principle

The Writer Canvas must support:

* **Creation** — "I have an idea."
* **Development** — "I am shaping something."
* **Renewal** — "I am bringing an older work back to life."
* **Collaboration** — "I am creating with others."
* **Scholarship** — "I am developing rigorous research."
* **Expression** — "I am sharing what I have created."

### The deeper architectural point

The Writer Canvas is not a "writing app." It is a **creative development environment**.
Its job is to help a person move any meaningful work from:

**potential → formation → expression**

whether that work is a book, a dissertation, a blog, a campaign, a teaching, or a revived
manuscript waiting for another chapter.

## The Central Design Question

How do we create one familiar environment while allowing radically different creative processes?

The answer cannot be:

> "Every mode gets its own app."

The answer must be:

> "Same studio. Different instruments."

## Where to Begin

Begin with the experience question:

> "What does it feel like to enter a professional creative studio?"

not:

> "What components do we need to build?"

**The first artifact should not be code. It should be a room map** — the permanent zones every
creator enters:

```
                 WRITER CANVAS

 ------------------------------------------------
| Context              | Work Field     | MAIA    |
|                      |                |         |
| Materials            | Create         | Reflect |
| Structure            | Develop        | Connect |
| History              | Write          | Explore |
 ------------------------------------------------

| Sources | Drafts | Versions | Collaborators |
 ------------------------------------------------
```

Then ask:

* What does every creator need here?
* What changes when the work becomes a book?
* What changes when it becomes a blog?
* What changes when it remains an idea?

Then, immediately after drawing the room map, walk five personas through it:

1. **The novelist** — "I have a manuscript."
2. **The scholar** — "I have a dissertation."
3. **The blogger/creator** — "I have an idea for a series."
4. **The collaborator** — "We are writing together."
5. **The elder/teacher** — "I have decades of notes and teachings."

**The design challenge — and the first real test: does one room support all of these without
becoming confusing?**

The next conversation should NOT start with database changes, new schemas, or feature lists.
The execution sequence is:

1. **Draw the room** — define the permanent zones; establish what every creator sees.
2. **Walk the room through the five creators** — the personas above are the first validation
   set, not a brainstorming aid. Without them the design will optimize for the most obvious
   case (the novelist) and narrow the Canvas back into a book-writing tool.
3. **Identify what is universal vs. what changes** — what every creator needs; what only
   certain modes require; what instruments appear conditionally.

   Starting hypotheses (to be tested by the persona walk, not assumed):

   * Universal: relationship to the Work · materials · creative field · orientation ·
     history · reflection.
   * Conditional instruments: chapters · citations · collaboration controls · publishing
     workflows · research structures · revision tools.

   Without this separation the system becomes either too generic ("a blank editor for
   everyone") or too fragmented ("a different app for every creator").
4. **Build the first slice.**

All five creators need a place for the work, materials, development space, reflection,
history, and expression — but they do not need the same instruments at the same time.

The conceptual expansion is complete — this brief is the ceiling, not a floor to keep raising.
