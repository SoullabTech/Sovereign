Study 004

Revision

Observations only. Nothing here is a recommendation.
What dies
  • Momentum. Drafting moves forward. Revision is stop–start by nature.
  • Permission to be wrong. Drafting defers with "I'll fix it later." Revision is later.
  • Discovery. You stop finding out what you think. You find out whether you said it.
  • One direction. Attention goes back three pages, forward to chapter nine, and out to the shape, often in the same minute.
  • Being alone with the sentence. Drafting holds one thing. Revision always holds at least two.
What appears
  • The reader. The writer tries to become someone who hasn't read it.
  • Distance, manufactured on purpose. Printing, reading aloud, waiting a week, changing the typeface — all techniques for becoming a stranger to your own text.
  • Structure. Invisible while drafting. Only exists once the whole exists.
  • Repetition. You start seeing your own tics — the word used six times a page.
  • Comparison. Against the earlier version, against what you meant, against the chapter before.
  • Whole-work doubt. Doesn't arise while drafting.
What writers actually do
  • Read aloud. The ear catches what the eye passes over.
  • Print it, then read it in a different room or chair.
  • Cross out instead of deleting — and keep looking at what was crossed out.
  • Spread pages on a table or the floor to see the sequence at once.
  • Draw arrows between things that are far apart.
  • Circle a phrase before knowing what's wrong with it.
  • Write "?" in the margin — the same gesture as drafting.
  • Keep a separate list of things to fix later, so revising doesn't stall.
  • Reread the last page aloud after one change, to hear whether it still holds.
Which gestures survive
WriteSurvives, meaning changed. Drafting adds. Revising replaces.
KeepSurvives, meaning inverted. Drafting: I'll want this later. Revising: this is right, don't touch it.
?Survives unchanged. Possibly the only one.
ReturnSplits in two. Where you were writing, and where you were reading, stop being the same place.
GroupStronger here than in drafting. Noticing that three scattered passages are one thing is a revision act.
Tensions
One mark, two opposite instructions. A writer will use Keep both ways in the same manuscript, months apart.
Deleting is not crossing out. A struck line is a decision you can still see. Undo is invisible.
Revision wants two things at once. A page shows one. Nearly every physical revision behavior is a way of seeing more than one thing at a time.
Writers add friction on purpose. Print, wait, read aloud. The instinct to remove friction may be wrong here.
The fix-later list lives outside the manuscript. It exists so revising can keep moving — and it is always a second document.
Reading aloud leaves no trace. The most common revision behavior produces nothing a system can see.
What surprised us
  • Keep inverted. Nobody expects a mark to reverse its meaning halfway through a book.
  • Writers add friction deliberately. Print it, wait a week, read it aloud. Removing friction may be the wrong instinct here.
  • Revision is plural. Drafting is singular. Revising always holds at least two things at once.
  • Crossing out is not deleting. Writers want to keep looking at what they decided against.

Not findings. Things that challenged an assumption and deserve more attention.

Questions
  • Is revision one practice or three? Polishing lines, reordering structure, and cutting may share nothing.
  • Does a revising writer want the drafting page at all, or the same text in a different form?
  • Where does the previous version live — beside, beneath, or gone?
  • Is distance the writer's job or the environment's?
  • What happens to a mark when the paragraph it marked is rewritten?
